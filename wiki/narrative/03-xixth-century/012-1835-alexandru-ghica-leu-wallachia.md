## 1835 📅 Alexandru Ghica 💰 Leu 🌍 Wallachia

<!-- u:u00268 kind:quad -->
The Introduction of the Leu as the Official Currency
Appointed by the Ottomans in agreement with the Russians after the end of the Russian occupation—at the same time Mihail Sturdza was named ruler of Moldavia—Alexandru Dimitrie Ghica became Prince of Wallachia. In 1835, during his reign, the principality took a pioneering step by introducing the leu as its official currency. On September 16, Ghica decreed the adoption of the leu as the unit of account, set at the value of 60 Turkish parale. This initiative arose in a period of monetary disorder, when numerous foreign coins circulated at once, complicating trade and taxation.
The choice of the name leu was not arbitrary. It came from the Dutch löwenthaler, a widely used silver coin in the 1700s that bore the image of a rampant lion (standing on its hind legs). For Romanians, this coin was so familiar that it had already become synonymous with stability and value, making it a natural candidate for the official unit of account. Similar influences appeared elsewhere in the Balkans, with both Albania and Bulgaria later naming their currencies after the same lion-taler.
Although symbolically important, the reform faced major limitations. The adoption of the leu was more theoretical than practical, as the Ottoman Empire—under whose suzerainty the Romanian principalities remained—refused to allow the minting of local coins. Thus, while the Wallachian leu existed as a unit of measure in official accounts and decrees, everyday commerce still depended on a mix of foreign currencies. In neighboring Moldavia, Prince Mihail Sturdza had similar plans for a national coinage, but his attempts were likewise blocked by the Ottomans, who viewed independent monetary policy as a step toward political autonomy.
The Ghica family, considered by some historians to have Albanian roots, gave several rulers to the Romanian Principalities and contributed prominent figures to politics, culture, and academia.
