# Calendar

<!-- u:u01499 kind:quad -->
Julian and Gregorian Weekdays
The days of the week in the Gregorian and Julian calendars remain the same because both calendars follow a continuous seven-day weekly cycle that has never been interrupted since its inception. However, the dates differ due to the way each calendar calculates leap years. The Julian calendar overestimates the length of the year, causing it to drift approximately one day every 128 years compared to the solar year. To correct this drift, the Gregorian calendar was introduced in 1582, which adjusted the leap year rules to better align with the solar year. By 1859, this difference had grown to 12 days, meaning that while the days of the week matched (e.g., a Wednesday in the Julian calendar was still a Wednesday in the Gregorian calendar), the Julian calendar's dates lagged behind by 12 days.
Austria adopted the Gregorian calendar shortly after Pope Gregory XIII's decree in 1583, along with the rest of the European Catholic world. Russia made the transition in 1918, the Ottoman Empire in 1917 (with full adoption in 1926), and Romania in 1919.
