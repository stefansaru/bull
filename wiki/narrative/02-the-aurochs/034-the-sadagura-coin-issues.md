## 🐃🦅💰 The Sadagura Coin Issues

<!-- u:u00185 kind:text -->
📅 XVIIIth century 🌍 Moldavia and Wallachia

<!-- u:u00186 kind:quad -->
🐃 The Sadagura Coin Issues
During the Russo-Turkish War (1768–1774), concluded with the Treaty of Küçük-Kainarji, Marshal P.A. Rumyantsev, commander of the Russian troops in Moldavia and Wallachia, initiated economic and fiscal reforms. Among these was the issuance of Sadagura coins, common currency for both Principalities, minted between 1771 and 1774 at the Sadagura mint near Chernivtsi, founded by Baron Nicolaus Gartenberg (Sadogurschi).
The bronze coins, with nominal values of 1 para / 3 denghi and 2 parale / 3 kopecks, displayed on their obverse the coats of arms of Moldavia and Wallachia, with values expressed in both the Turkish and Russian monetary systems. The primary purpose of issuing these coins was to address the shortage of small change, while also aiming to familiarize the local population with the Russian monetary system and an imperially controlled exchange rate.
For the minting of these coins, Baron Gartenberg received bronze sourced from cannons captured during the war, primarily Turkish cannons, but also a few defective Russian ones. Some cannons even came from Poland, specifically from the Polonne fortress, and were declared to be of Turkish origin. The diverse sources of metal resulted in variations in the quality of the Sadagura coins.
A distinctive feature of these coins is that they represent a Russian monetary issue intended simultaneously for two different states, bearing the inscription “MOLDAVIAN AND WALLACHIAN COIN”.
The introduction of bronze coins in Moldavia and Wallachia was met with resistance by the population, accustomed to coins made of gold and silver. Bronze coins, previously associated with counterfeits, were viewed with suspicion.
Although they effectively created a monetary union between the two Principalities, the excessive coin supply between 1772 and 1774 quickly overwhelmed the market's capacity to absorb them. As the war neared its end and Russian troops began withdrawing, merchants refused to accept the bronze coins, favoring precious metal currencies instead.
Russia attempted to enforce the circulation of Sadagura coins through strict orders, compelling merchants to accept them directly from soldiers, without room for negotiation.
This system created a mechanism of economic exploitation: the local treasuries were forced to accept Sadagura coins, pay the Russian army in gold and silver, and circulate the depreciated coins locally. In the end, the population was left with worthless currency, while gold and silver flowed into Russian hands.
