# Famous collectors of the Bull's Heads

> A collection index. Part of the *romance* of the Moldavian *cap de bour* (1858)
> is the roll-call of legendary cabinets the stamps have passed through. This page
> narrates the great collectors, how their holdings were dispersed, and why an
> "ex-" line in a modern catalogue still moves the price. See also
> [[../concepts/scarcity-of-bull-heads]], [[../concepts/bull-heads-today]],
> [[../references/bulls-head-lots]] and the census [[../postmarks/census]].
> Provenance-drives-value is a headline finding of the
> [[../../strategy/market-research|market research]] [3].

Because only a few hundred Bull's Heads survive — a modern census reconciles to
roughly 714, "fewer than 800," of which about 100 are on cover [1][10] — the same
pieces recur across a century of famous cabinets. Their journeys are unusually
well documented: nearly every important example carries a chain of named owners
and dated sales in its catalogue provenance [2]. What follows is the great line of
custodians, in roughly chronological order of their dispersals.

## Philippe de Ferrary — the greatest of all

**Philippe la Renotière von Ferrary** (1850–1917) assembled what is generally
called the most complete worldwide collection ever formed, funded by an inherited
fortune of some 120 million francs and stored at the Hôtel Matignon in Paris,
where he bought "many important old collections" wholesale [4]. Holding Austrian
citizenship, he was deemed a technical enemy of France; after his death the
collection was seized as war reparations under the Treaty of Versailles (effective
January 1920) and auctioned in **fourteen sales in Paris between 1921 and 1926**,
realising about 30 million francs [4]. Ferrary is reported to have held on the
order of **32 Bull's Head stamps** among his Romania (exact figure varies between
sources) `(needs-ref)` [5]. His collection is the ultimate origin point of many
present-day pedigrees: an "ex-Ferrary" line still "considerably raises the
desirability and value" of a rarity today [5], the market mechanism formalised in
[[../../strategy/market-research|market research]] [3]. Most emblematically, the
**unique used 27-parale tête-bêche pair** — unseen since 1950 — passed through
Ferrary and then Arthur Hind before entering King Carol II's holdings [5][1].

## Arthur Hind

**Arthur Hind** (d. 1933), the American textile magnate best known for owning the
British Guiana 1c magenta, was a lover of classic "primitives." His Romania was
sold as **Part 8** of the dispersal of his collection — auctions held in New York
and London — offered in **London in 1935 as 55 lots** [1]. Hind material,
including the tête-bêche pair noted above, subsequently "join[ed] King Carol's
holdings" [1][5].

## King Carol II of Romania

The Bull's Heads were, in the phrase of the period, part of "the hobby of kings":
the Romanian source lists **King George V of Britain, King Carol II of Romania and
King Farouk of Egypt** among the crowned philatelists [9]. **Carol II** owned the
national treasures directly — most famously the unique used 27-parale tête-bêche
pair, which he exhibited in 1950 [1][5]. His royal collection later **disappeared**
and remains one of the great unresolved losses of the field [10]. The formation of
that royal cabinet, and the role of court figures such as Eugeniu Buhman, is
treated at length in the Romanian collecting history [9].

## Baron J. W. von Witzleben

A Bucharest banker (linked to the family of the July 1944 anti-Hitler plotter),
**Baron von Witzleben** owned, per an inventory, one cover of each first-issue
value *except* the 81 parale, and was strong in second-issue rarities. His
collection was **seized by Soviet forces at the close of the Second World War** and
lost [1] — the confiscation is documented in the Romanian source as a set-piece
episode [9], and echoes the broader pattern of material vanishing eastward in 1945
[3][10].

## King Farouk of Egypt

**King Farouk**'s vast holdings — "possibly the largest stamp collection in the
world" — were **forcibly sold in Cairo in 1954** after his overthrow, in the
three-day *Palace Collections of Egypt* sale run by Harmers on behalf of the new
Egyptian government at the Kubbeh Palace [1][8]. His Romania included Bull's Head
material; a 108-parale used from Galatz that later entered "a famous Swiss
collection" is traced in the Feldman collector-history to a cabinet dispersed in
that 1954 Cairo sale [1] (the Swiss holder is not publicly named `(needs-ref)`).

## Alfred H. Caspary

**Alfred H. Caspary** (d. 1955) formed what was "considered the finest collection
of the first issue ever formed — **31 stamps and 9 covers**" [1]. It was sold by
**H. R. Harmer in New York**, the Romania appearing in the **1957** series (the
Romania section is the sale cited in later provenances as "Caspary, 1957, Sale 11")
[1][2][11]. Caspary lots are among the most frequently invoked pedigrees in the
census: for example the **27 parale unused** "unquestionably the finest in
existence," Caspary Sale 11 lot 346; a **108 parale on cover** (Caspary Sale 11 lot
365); and a **5 parale mint block of four** (Caspary Sale 11 lot 405) — each then
onward to later owners [2]. Tie these to specific records in
[[../references/bulls-head-lots]].

## Maurice Burrus

**Maurice Burrus**, the Alsatian tobacco magnate, held a substantial first issue —
"**7 covers and 17 single stamps plus a pair of the 27 Parale (ex Caspary)**" — and
the celebrated second-issue newspaper franked with eight 5-parale stamps. His
collection was **sold in Belgium in 1962** [1]. Burrus provenances recur in the
census: a superb **108 parale on pink** with the Fokschani datestamp (Burrus 1962,
lot 197) and a **54 parale on cover** to Galatz (ex Burrus 1962, lot 191, thence
Hassel and Tomasini) are documented examples [2].

## John E. du Pont

**John E. du Pont** (1938–2010) was a top-flight philatelist — winner of **five FIP
Grand Prix awards (1978–1986)** and the anonymous 1980 buyer of the British Guiana
1c magenta for $935,000 [7]. After his death (in prison) his stamps were sold by
**David Feldman**; his British Guiana alone made €6.5 million in **June 2014** [7].
Bull's Head material "from the collection of John du Pont" featured in Feldman's
**May 2014 Geneva** auction — including an **81/108 parale on pink** unused example
recorded "ex du Pont" (Heimbüchler census no. 378/2) [2][7].

## Georges Tomasini and the modern custodians

Several twentieth-century cabinets bridge the classic era to today:

- **Alfred F. Lichtenstein** and his daughter **Louise Boyd Dale** assembled world
  rarities; their Romania went to auction in **1966 in 50 lots** [1].
- **John R. Boker, Jr.**, the German-States authority, built a quality Romania
  "founded on purchases from the Rothschild collection," largely sold privately [1].
  Rothschild pedigrees appear directly in the census (e.g. an 80-parale unique
  variety and a tête-bêche pair) [2].
- **Eduardo Cohen**, a Lisbon-based figure in Romanian oil, gathered an important
  first issue (a 27-parale pair, six used 108 parale, an unused 81 parale) that won
  the Grand Prix at LISBOA 1953 and BARCELONA 1960 and was **sold from 1967** [1].
- **Georges Tomasini** (Tomasini-Kunzi) acquired key items **from Caspary and
  Burrus** — most notably the eight-5-parale newspaper — and carefully preserved the
  collection; "Tomasini, 1969" is one of the single most common provenance lines in
  the census [1][2]. Other noted twentieth-century holdings include those of
  W. Dorning Beckton, George Worthington, René Poncelet, Consul Alfred Weinberger,
  Hans Lagerloef, Sig Adler, Lars Amundsen, René Berlingin and John Hall [1].

More recent chains recorded in the lots data include **Conte Contini** (1966),
**Leon** (1966), **Robineau** (1960), and **Robson Lowe** offerings, alongside the
**Hausman collection** dispersed by Feldman in December 2022 (with pieces earlier
held by Robson Lowe and traced through Mohrmann, Köhler and Steltzer in
Heimbüchler) [2][6]. For living owners, only what appears in public auction records
is stated here, by design [3].

## Why provenance drives value

Two forces make an "ex-" line load-bearing rather than decorative. First,
**scarcity plus a thin, expert market**: with the serious global collector base in
the low hundreds, the *same* documented pieces resurface, so a named pedigree is
often the difference between a reference example and an uncertain one
([[../concepts/scarcity-of-bull-heads]]) [3]. Second, the **forgery problem**: the
Bull's Heads are among the most-forged classics (Fournier, Sperati, the domestic
forger Moroiu, and the 2009 National Philatelic Museum substitution scandal), so a
chain of ownership through Caspary, Burrus or Ferrary — reinforced by a **Heimbüchler
census number and certificate** — functions as authentication as much as romance
[3][10]. The market bears this out: recent five- and six-figure results attach
overwhelmingly to well-pedigreed lots, and the field's record item stands near
€750k–830k [3]. The role of the **expertiser** (Heimbüchler above all, with earlier
signatures such as Diena, Calves, Lemaire, Pfenninger, Richter, Bloch and Gmach on
individual pieces) is the connective tissue of every strong provenance [2][3].

## Losses and gaps

The counterpoint to these great cabinets is how much has vanished: **King Carol II's
royal collection disappeared**; the **von Witzleben** holdings were seized by Soviet
troops in 1945; a group destined for a 1988 English auction was **stolen at a London
airport and never resurfaced**; and pieces sit locked in museums and a "famous Swiss
collection" outside the market [10][3][1]. These gaps are exactly why the surviving
documented pedigrees are so prized. See [[../concepts/bull-heads-today]] for the
present state of the census and market, and [[../references/README]] for how these
provenance claims are sourced.
