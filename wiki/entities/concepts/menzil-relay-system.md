# The menzil relay post — how mail moved before 1858

Long before the *Cap de bour* stamps of July 1858, the Principalities were
crossed by a chain of horse-relays whose vocabulary was Turco-Slavic and whose
logic was that of a state courier service, not a public letter post. This page
goes below the surface of that system — the *menzil* stations, the men who ran
them, the vehicles and warrants, the toll called *ugerit*, and above all the
**farming-out of the whole apparatus to private entrepreneurs**, the Greek
contractor **C. Scufa** foremost among them. It is the institution the 1850s
reforms had to dismantle; those reforms — the 1852 uniform tariff, the 1857 state
buy-back — are treated in [[postal-history-romania]] and only cross-referenced
here. For the political frame, see [[geopolitics-1800s]].

## The station: menzil, menzilhanea, căpitan de menzil

From the early 18th century the roads carried a network of relay posts "called
**menziluri**, run by a **căpitan de menzil**, where the horses and the *olace*
were kept, to carry onward the couriers of the State, officials on mission and
persons authorised by the Prince to travel by post"
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.117). The
phrasing is exact: the beneficiaries named are couriers, officials and the
Prince's licensees — the private letter-writer is not among them. A 1739 order of
Grigore Ghica, for instance, instructs "the captains of the relays to furnish
*olace* with four horses each, saddle-horses and *surugii*, from stage to stage"
(*din conac în conac*) for two French envoys and Ottoman dignitaries bound for
Hotin
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.118).

The station-house itself was the **menzilhanea**. A detailed princely ordinance
reproduced in the history sets out its internal government: at every
*menzilhanea* a "captain of the men" (*căpetenie de slujitori*) was also the
**menzilgi-bași**, keeper and manager of that relay; the frontier captain issued
the incomer his **carte de menzil** (relay pass); a **logofăt** placed under the
*vel căpitan de menziluri* entered in the *condică* (register) the name of
everyone who came and went, and whether they travelled "with *ugerit* or without
*ugerit*"
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.122). Weekly
each captain was to send a clean list of all who had passed his station
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.123). It was a
transport-and-toll bureaucracy for authorised travellers; letters rode along as
a by-product.

## The vehicles: olac versus diligență

The characteristic vehicle of the old post was the **olac** — a small light
carriage (the word also stands for the relay-run itself), harnessed with four
horses for a fast courier stage and kept ready at each *menzilhanea*
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.117–118). It
was built for speed and officialdom, not comfort or capacity. The systematic
covered **diligență** (stagecoach) came much later and from the West: the first
real diligence service in Moldavia put "sprung, systematic carriages" at
travellers' disposal and enlarged what the post could carry
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.182), and "the
example set by Moldavia, with the establishment of a diligence service, was
imitated at once in Wallachia, where in April 1852 (O.S.) the first diligence began
running on the Bucharest–Giurgiu line"
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.183). The move
from *olac* to *diligență* is also the move from a courier relay toward a
passenger-and-mail service — the seed of the 1852 "Directorate of the Diligences"
that [[postal-history-romania]] describes.

## The drivers, the warrant and the toll

Three things governed a journey by post: the **surugii**, the **podorojnă** and
the **ugerit**. The *surugii* were the postillion-drivers who rode the horses;
in the 1795 arrangement they were to be free unmarried men, tax-exempt, waged,
and marked with a lead seal of the princely arms to tell them from ordinary
taxpayers
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.122).

To travel one needed a warrant. Those setting out from towns were issued
**cărți Domnești** (princely letters); those from the districts took
*teșcherele* from the *ispravnici*
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.128). Under
Russian influence the same warrant appears by its Slavonic name, the
**podorojnă**: a contract clause of 1811 provides that "for Russian *podorojni*,
in conformity with the imperial order of 6 July 1808 (O.S.)," couriers were to be
given extra horses without charge
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.135). The word
outlived the old post: as late as 9 September 1852 (O.S.) the Moldavian
authorities still spoke of "the regulation permit issued by the post
(*podorojnă*)," now supplemented by a stamped *blanchet* naming the traveller,
the distance and the number of horses
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.182).

The **ugerit** was the relay toll, levied per horse per hour and — tellingly —
graded by rank. In the 1795 rules the drivers took an *ugerit* of 5 bani a horse
from boyars and merchants, sparing the peasantry, a fee that fed the *surugii*
themselves; on the same schedule couriers paid an *ugerit* of 10 bani an hour,
caftaned boyars 15, merchants and others 20 per horse
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.128). The older
ordinance sets the norm at "10 bani per hour for each horse," collected by the
frontier captain unless the traveller held a *ferman* or a princely exemption, in
which case the pass was registered but not charged
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.122). Access was
policed, too: the public's permission to use the post "was subordinated to an
authorisation from the local authorities" certifying the applicant was no fugitive,
issued only by the Hatman in Bucharest, by *ispravnici* in the county capitals, and
by rural authorities below them
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.128).

## A farmed-out service — and the entrepreneur C. Scufa

The decisive fact about this post is that the state seldom ran it directly. It
was leased — in the language of the ordinances, given "în antrepriză" to
**contracii** (contractors) — for fixed terms. When Alexandru Moruzi took the
Moldavian service in hand at the end of the 18th century he "gave it in
enterprise to a company headed by a Greek, C. Scufa"
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.127). The
Wallachian *hrisov* of 1 May 1795 (O.S.) that Moruzi then issued reads like a
lease of the whole machine: the treasury handed over 700 horses free of charge,
ordered the *menzilhanele* repaired with guest-rooms, stables and barley-stores,
and bound the *contracii* to keep the horses, drivers and buildings intact to the
end of the term
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.127). Under
such a lease the personnel split in two: the **State** appointed the *Hatman al
poștelor* (a kind of director-general), the *beșlii* (guards) and the *surugii*,
while the **enterprise** appointed the *căpitanii de poștă* and the men who ran
and exploited the service day to day
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.128).

Scufa is the thread that runs through the whole period. In Moldavia "the postal
service, which had been given in enterprise to Scufa ever since 1792, was kept up
rather better" than in Wallachia
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.130). When the
Russian occupation of 1806–1812 threw the Wallachian post into disorder,
negotiations reopened "with Scufa, the same man who had already held the
enterprise of the post in Wallachia," who on 9 May 1811 (O.S.) formed a
partnership to work the posts with **Polizache Dimitrie, the treasurer Moscu, the
Clucer Deșliu and the Russian councillor Varlam**; the war-time service leaned on
outside credit — the merchant **Manuc Bei**, of
[[../places/hanul-lui-manuc|Hanul lui Manuc]] fame, gave 60,000 lei and lent a
further 100,000 interest-free
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.133).

When the war ended with the peace of Bucharest of 26 May 1812 (O.S.), the
retreating Russians left the enterprise unpaid, and Scufa — "as a Russian
subject" — pressed the new Prince Ion Caragea for 301,673 taleri of Russian
arrears and later a further 375,000 in damages, while Deșliu claimed 84,000 and
Varlam demanded the return of his invested capital
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.136). The
government rescinded the old lease and signed a fresh one — again with Scufa,
though with fewer horses on every route — then farmed the post to him once more
"for a term of four years, that is from 1814 to 1818"
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.136–137). And
because in Moldavia too a consortium ceded the concession to him in 1817, "Scufa,
who also held the enterprise of the Wallachian posts," ended up controlling both:
"so that in the last years of which we speak, the postal service of both
Principalities was found reunited under a single management"
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.137). One Greek
entrepreneur, in effect, held the mails of the future Romania.

## The seals the relay houses left

The menzil system is not only a documentary abstraction — it left **physical
markings**. The seals of the **Casa Menzilului Moldovei** are the oldest postal
devices Kiriac Dragomir catalogues, and they run in step with the very leases
described above. The earliest, **[[../handstamps/SM22|SM22]]** (1822), survives as a
single known impression on a postal order of 7 September 1825 issued under
*"trienalul antreprizei Scufa, 1822/25"* — Scufa's lease made tangible
([[../../sources/dragomir-stampilografie-1990/text|Dragomir, Ștampilografie]] photo
11). Successive three-year terms brought new dies: **[[../handstamps/SM27|SM27]]**
(1827, a double circle showing a postillion sounding a horn between two trees) and
**[[../handstamps/SM30|SM30]]** (1830, already bearing the bull's-head arms). The
Cyrillic legend *"PECETEA KĂSII MENZILULUI MOLDAVIEI"* names the institution this
page describes. The full series, and the Wallachian counterpart (*Casa Menzilurilor
Valahiei*, [[../handstamps/README|SV27 ff.]]), is catalogued at
[[pre-stamp-postal-markings]].

## Why the system bred its own reform

The farmed-out relay post carried within it the pressures that would destroy it.
It was built for the wrong customer — its charter beneficiaries were couriers and
officials, and ordinary use required a policing authorisation
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.117, p.128) — and
it was manipulated for economy at the public's expense: Prince Hangerliu, to spend
less on fodder, banned horse exports until he could buy cheap and "restricted the
right of those who wished to travel by post"
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.130). It was
prone to sudden contraction, too: the reduction of horses that "cut the public off
from transport by post gave rise to discontents," forcing the Divan to take the
service in hand
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.136), while the
loss of [[../events/1812-treaty-of-bucharest|Bessarabia in 1812]] shrank Moldavia
and with it the relay establishment, whose horses fell toward 1,200
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.137).

A post that served the powerful, taxed by rank, restricted the public and hung on
the solvency of a Greek contractor could never become a cheap, universal letter
service. The remedy was to take it back into the State's hands and re-price it for
the ordinary letter — the uniform 40-parale tariff of 1852, the 1857 buy-back of
Teodor Ghica's diligence right, and finally the 1858 reform that put a **postage
stamp** in the sender's hand (see [[postal-history-romania]] and
[[foreign-post-offices]]). The *Cap de bour* was, in the end, the small paper
token by which the letter-writer at last replaced the *căpitan de menzil*.
