# Authenticating the Bull's Heads — Wertheimer-Ghika vs. the "fantasy stamps"

Where [[forgeries|the forgery page]] follows the *forgers* — Moroiu's press,
Sperati's reproductions, Fournier's facsimiles, and the physical tells that betray
them — this page follows a very different kind of authentication. In 1944 the
collector **Jacques Wertheimer-Ghika (W.-G.)** published *Documentele Poștei Române*
("The Documents of the Romanian Post"), a book built not on comparing stamps but on
the **official postal archive**. His claim is that the genuineness of a Moldavian
*Cap de bour* piece — and above all of its **postmark** — can be settled by tying it
to a **dated document in the files of the Directorate of Posts**, and that most of
the doubt swirling around genuine used Bull's Heads was manufactured by writers who
had never seen those files. This is offered here as **his position**, and a polemical
one (DR-006): the book is as much an indictment of his contemporaries as it is a study.

## The claim, and the polemic that frames it

W.-G. opens the Preface by declaring that his book breaks "the rut in which Romanian
philately had grown used, in recent years, to standing still." He argues that because
collectors believed the postal files of Moldavia and Muntenia were **lost**, and never
bothered to look for them, "a literature grew up, appreciable in volume, negligible in
value," in which everything written about the history of the stamps "is nothing but the
result of a fertile imagination on the part of the respective authors, **devoid of any
documentary basis**" ([[../../sources/wertheimer-ghika-1944/text|Wertheimer-Ghika 1944]]
photo 5). He extends the charge to named foreign writers — "Brunel, Poncelet, Moens,
Becton and Duerst" — whose errors he says at least have "the excuse of distance, by
thousands of miles, from the source of truth, which our archives constitute," and more
harshly to the resident writers "v. Witzleben and Zoscsak," who lived "in our midst" yet
published discoveries "without possessing or knowing the documentary proofs for their
assertions" (photo 5).

The book's second, sharper target is what he calls the **"mărci fanteziste"** —
fantasy/fabricated stamps. His central exhibit is a scholarly one: a 1906 Bucharest work,
*Studiu asupra Monopolurilor în România* by **Th. C. Aslan** (photo 5), honoured by the
Romanian Academy with the G. San-Marin prize and printed at the Academy's expense, which
— he says — reproduced at page 61 "the **fantasy stamps**, the product of a sick brain,
fabricated by a known forger, Moroiu, prosecuted for forgery — as our first and genuine
postage stamps, issued in the year 1854!" (photo 6). For W.-G. the scandal is that "false stamps
circulated under the shield of the Romanian Academy" (photo 6) — the same forger, Moroiu,
whom the [[forgeries|forgery page]] treats as the emblematic domestic counterfeiter,
here dressed up as history by an official institution.

## What "authentication through the document trail" means

W.-G.'s method is the inverse of catalogue study. He states that Part I of the book is
"a faithful rendering of the state of the posts in Moldova at the time the first Romanian
stamps appeared, from **official reports and files**, illustrated with pieces from the
author's collection," so that "all the data are therefore easy to check" (photo 7). A
marking is authenticated not by how closely it *resembles* a reference strike, but by
finding the **archival act that proves the device existed, at that office, on that date** —
and then matching a surviving cover or stamp to it. Physical state is corroboration, not
the primary proof: he notes that progressive **die and postmark defects** — a cracked
frame, a broken outer circle — appear only after a cliché "had already been in use for
2½ years without the defect," so such flaws are "**very important for establishing
authenticity, as well as the date of use**" (photo 80, pp. 152–153). Combined with a
recorded schedule of the **months and years each device was used** and "the list of the
ink colour," he asserts that "no forger, however advanced," can evade detection, so that
"a forgery is no longer hard to discover" (photo 36).

This is a genuinely different axis of authentication from the plating-and-expertise
tradition summarised on the [[forgeries]] and [[../postmarks/census|postmark census]]
pages. It also runs against the grain of the catalogue-based scholarship traced in
[[early-philatelic-study]]: for W.-G., a catalogue that has not been checked against the
files is precisely the problem, not the authority.

## The fabrications and errors he denounces

W.-G. does not merely disagree; he names people, and treats their claims as damage. He
says publications "completely lacking objectivity," together with "rumours and whispers
that condemned and depreciated **authentic and rare pieces**… as 'falsified,'" dripped
"their poison, slowly but surely, into the ears of credulous collectors," and concludes
"**much harm has Romanian philately suffered at the hands of these uncalled-for
slanderers!**" (photo 6). Refusing "conventional silence," he lists as culprits **Const.
Minescu** (a P.T.T. pensioner), the lawyers **Gr. Racoviceanu** and **I. Bogdan** — then
president, vice-president and committee member of the *Societatea Filatelică Română* — and
the dealers **Geza v. Jakots, Onig Tulbendjan** and **Ion Odor**, charging that they
posed as experts of the old stamps and postmarks without "the massive comparative
material strictly necessary for any expertise, and especially for **the expertising of
postmarks**" (photo 7). His Preface ends on the deliberately theatrical note
"**Șarlatania a murit, trăiască filatelia română!!**" ("Charlatanry is dead, long live
Romanian philately!!"), dated February 1944 (photo 7). This attribution — his verdict on
named contemporaries — is his polemic, not an adjudicated finding, and should be read as
such.

## Worked examples: contested postmarks vindicated

The book's authentication engine is clearest where a dismissed postmark is run back
through the files.

- **Adjud.** Assertions that certain station postmarks did not exist were, W.-G. says,
  "proclaimed and published with some carelessness in the *Revista Societății Filatelice
  Române*," yet "without the named parties bringing a single proof, or showing a single
  document" (photo 30). Against them he sets report No. 975 (1 May 1858) and No. 995
  (Section 3), which show that some **passenger-post stations also handled letters, sold
  stamps and had their own cancelling devices** — and a Focsani office stock-list of
  26 September 1858 records stamps sent to such "outside expeditors." The Adjud station,
  he writes, "received its postal postmark already in 1858, and one which only now, after
  86 years, I have been able to find" — vindicating "postmarks used authentically at the
  time, but whose existence was, with much noise and without any proof, **contested by
  ignorant people**" (photo 40).
- **The 1872/73 date table.** He prints a two-column comparison of office-opening dates:
  the "inexact" dates from the official *Darea de Seamă a Direcțiunei Generale a
  Telegrafelor și Poștelor pe anii 1872/73* against the "exact" dates "from the original
  files of the Directorate of Posts of Moldavia, 1858/62" — e.g. Adjud given as 1864
  where the files say 1858. Had the files stayed lost, he argues, philately "would remain
  deprived of some of the most beautiful and rare pieces cancelled with those postmarks,
  which 'our eminent' Minescu had **declared non-existent or false**," in the Society's
  Review of 1942 and 1943 (photo 46, near 4 Nov 1858).
- **Tg. Frumos and Reni.** The Tg. Frumos office "was opened in 1858 and received its
  postmark in the first months of 1859… **I possess in my collection letters with the
  Tg. Frumos postmark of 1859!**" — an office "whose existence in this period is contested
  and whose postmark is declared forged, again by these gentlemen Minescu and Racoviceanu"
  (photo 76, pp. 144–145). On the same pages he rebuts a collector (Mr. Mateescu) who
  declared a strike false on the theory that all digits of a datestamp must be identical:
  W.-G. explains the numerals were partly single-piece and partly assembled from separate
  figures, so they legitimately differ — "with what ease some condemn and declare a
  postmark forged, lacking the necessary knowledge" (photo 76). He notes the Reni office
  device, found in its box, proves that office existed "even though to this day no letters
  or stamps bearing this postmark have been found" (photo 76).

He extends the same logic to the remainders: the "most fanciful stories" about surviving
stock, spread "for years by the uninitiated, and especially by old postal officials such
as C. Minescu," are answered from correspondence in the files (photo 73, pp. 138–139).

## Why it matters — and why it stays `debated`

The stakes are practical. W.-G. himself frames the payoff in terms of scarcity: the 5
Parale with a broken frame, dated by its issue (December 1861) and withdrawal (April
1862), "**can be considered the rarest among the Cap de Bou stamps of both issues** — of
course, on condition of the authenticity of the postmark!" (photo 92, pp. 176–177). That
last clause is the whole argument in miniature: with a genuine used Bull's Head the
*postmark* is where value and doubt both live, and W.-G.'s wager is that the postal
archive can retire the doubt where catalogue lore only compounded it — the confidence
that underwrites today's market in genuine used examples (see [[scarcity-of-bull-heads]]).

None of this is neutral, and the page marks it as `debated` on purpose. W.-G. writes as
an interested collector whose own holdings gain if the "contested" postmarks are declared
genuine; his verdicts on named contemporaries are accusations, not settled history; and
his documentary confidence ("a forgery is no longer hard to discover") sits beside a
century of hard-won evidence that the Bull's Heads are among the most skilfully forged of
all classics — Sperati reproduced cancellations too (see [[forgeries]]). The durable
contribution is the **method**: authenticate a marking by pinning it to a dated act in
the Directorate's files, not by resemblance alone. The individual verdicts are his; the
method is what the field kept.
