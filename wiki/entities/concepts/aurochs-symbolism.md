# 🐃 Symbolism of the aurochs

> An ancient emblem becomes a nation's first postal voice. When Moldavia printed its
> first stamps in 1858, it did not invent an image — it reached for the oldest thing it
> owned: the head of the **aurochs** (Romanian *cap de bour*), the beast whose severed,
> forward-facing head had stood for the land since the fourteenth century. This page
> explains what that head **means** — heraldically, politically, spiritually — and why
> putting it on the first stamp was the natural, patriotic choice. For the founder-hunt
> story that mythologises its origin, see [[../events/dragos-founding-moldova]].

## The emblem in one line

The recognised arms of Moldavia are an **aurochs's head, seen frontally, with a star
between its horns**, the head accompanied by a **flower (lily) 🌸** on one flank and a
**crescent moon 🌙** on the other [[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/002-symbol-of-moldavia|Symbol of Moldavia]].
Tudor-Radu Tiron names this figure directly as *"capul de bour — însemn heraldic al
Principatului dintre Carpaţi şi Nistru"* — the aurochs head, heraldic emblem of the
Principality between the Carpathians and the Dniester
[[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.7. It is, the book adds,
"the most important symbol of the country," perpetuated over the centuries
[[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/002-symbol-of-moldavia|Symbol of Moldavia]].

Heraldically the figure is disciplined and consistent. Its basic traits are a **frontal
representation, usually without a neck**, which may be accompanied by auxiliary charges —
"firmament pieces" such as stars and the crescent, but also fleurs-de-lis or crosses
[[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.9. The head belongs to the
category of *animate* or "ensouled" heraldic figures, to which scholars have supposed a
**totemic origin** [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.9.

## What the animal signifies: the hunted king

The meaning of the aurochs head is, at root, the meaning of the **hunt**. In the
thirteenth- and fourteenth-century forests of Maramureş and the lands east of the
Carpathians, the aurochs (*bour*) was the "superlative" of every royal and seigneurial
chase — a local **king of the animals**, the great quarry reserved in principle to the
ruling class [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.9. To pursue
and fell such a beast was proof of physical prowess and of social rank; the hunt was
followed by the display of the trophy — the hide, head or horns — and, for the medieval
mind, "the assumption of the hunting trophy as a personal emblem appears as a natural way
of creating a coat of arms" [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.9.

So the aurochs head carries a compact bundle of meanings: **strength and the wild land**
(the beast that ruled the forest), **legitimacy and lordship** (the right to hunt it was a
feudal right, the trophy a mark of authority), and finally **the territory itself** — the
emblem of the man who founded the state became the emblem of the state. Tiron's governing
principle is that a country's earliest emblem must be that of its founder: *"a vorbi despre
cel dintâi însemn al Moldovei înseamnă a vorbi despre cel al întemeietorului ei"*
[[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.12.

## The attributes and their meanings

- **⭐ The star between the horns.** A "firmament piece," the star is the constant
  companion of the *bour* [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.9.
  Iorga already spoke of *"stema bourului însemnat cu steaua"* — the arms of the aurochs
  marked with the star [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.6.
- **🌸 The flower / lily.** On the earliest coins the head is flanked by a fleur-de-lis,
  and the Angevin lily reached the region with the **House of Anjou**, which used it in its
  own arms [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.9. On the first
  Moldavian money the aurochs is paired with a shield of **bars and fleurs-de-lis** that
  points unmistakably to the Angevin monarchy
  [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] pp.15–16.
- **🌙 The crescent moon.** Another firmament charge flanking the head
  [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.9. Its placement migrates —
  left on the coin, right on the seal of Roman I — a detail that lets scholars trace the
  evolution of the design [[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/028-seal-of-roman-i|Seal of Roman I]].
- **The rose.** The heraldic rose that traditionally accompanies the *cap de bour* is, in
  Tiron's argument, likewise a **Maramureş inheritance** — probably the emblem of the rival
  **Pok (Póki) clan**, whose arms were a rose, adopted into Bogdan's heraldic patrimony.
  Tellingly, adding a rose to the arms of Câmpulung-la-Tisa yields *exactly* the formula of
  Moldavia's arms as valid to this day
  [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] pp.18–19.

## Is it an aurochs or a wisent? `debated`

Whether the *cap de bour* depicts the true **aurochs** (*Bos primigenius*) or the European
**wisent / zimbru** (*Bison bonasus*) is an old and unresolved question. Tiron treats the
species as indifferent to the heraldry — *"indiferent de specia pe care o va fi avut acest
bovideu — zimbru sau bour"* — and notes the figure survives across variants (bour, zimbru,
taur) while keeping its basic traits
[[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] pp.8–9. On the seal of Stephen
the Great the horns even "resemble those of a European bison (wisent)," yet this need not
mean a wisent was intended: heraldry drifts stylistically over time
[[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/029-stephen-the-great|Stephen the Great]]. The confusion
deepened as the aurochs itself went extinct and artists increasingly drew the surviving
wisent instead [[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/002-symbol-of-moldavia|Symbol of Moldavia]].
See the dedicated page [[aurochs-vs-wisent]].

> A caution on deep antiquity. Tempting as it is to trace the emblem to prehistoric bull
> protomes (attested from the Eneolithic to the Iron Age), Tiron rejects these as credible
> sources: the "causal link" cannot be proven across the intervening millennia, and the
> so-called "arms of Dacia" is a late invention with no ancient basis. The emblem "cannot
> be sought in a period earlier than the appearance of the heraldic phenomenon" itself
> [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.11.

## The founding legend

> [!legend]
> Tradition holds that **Dragoş**, hunting a great aurochs down the valley that would take
> its name from his drowned hound *Molda*, cornered and slew the beast; its head was set
> *"spre pomenire … într-un par pe malul râului"* — "for remembrance, on a stake by the
> river's bank" (Miron Costin, 1677)
> [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.20. This is legend, not
> documented fact: the hunting tale was fashioned both to fix a *terminus a quo* for
> Moldavia's beginnings and to **justify its historic arms**
> [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.20. It is told in full,
> and weighed, at [[../events/dragos-founding-moldova]].

The kernel of truth the legend dramatizes is the transfer of a **Maramureş hunting emblem**
east of the Carpathians. Two of the five privileged Maramureş towns — Sighet and
Câmpulung-la-Tisa — already bore an aurochs head, the oldest attestation being a document
of **1383**, in which Ioan Mihály de Apşa read the head as the *"marca oraşului,"* the mark
of the town, and noted its coincidence with the arms of Moldavia
[[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] pp.7–8. As early as 1677 the
chronicler **Miron Costin** had observed that Sighet, capital of Maramureş, "to this day
has as its seal the aurochs head, like that country (Moldavia)"
[[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.6.

## Continuity across the centuries: seals from Roman I to Stephen the Great

The emblem's authority rests on unbroken use. Tiron works from a **presumption of
continuity** of the princely emblems, concluding that Moldavian dynastic heraldry "traces
its origins back to Dragoş," and that the later rulers of the Bogdăneşti line "did nothing
but preserve what was well established"
[[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.18. The material record bears
this out:

- **The Drăgoşeşti arms** — arrow, crescent and two stars — belong to the founding family
  and the founding period [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.12.
- **The Eski-Serai two-handed sword** carries, on its pommel, the Drăgoşeşti shield on one
  face and an **aurochs head with outward-curving horns** on the other — the "key" piece
  proving that both emblems travelled together
  [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.13.
- **The groat of Petru I Muşat** (r. 1375–1391) shows the same formula — aurochs head on
  the obverse, Angevin shield (bars and lilies) on the reverse — and these are among the
  oldest surviving representations of the Moldavian arms
  [[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/027-the-groat|The groat]];
  [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.13.
- **The seal of Roman I** (1392) fixes the head with its flower and crescent on a princely
  charter [[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/028-seal-of-roman-i|Seal of Roman I]].
- **Stephen the Great** (r. 1457–1504) carried the aurochs with star and crescent onto the
  seals and the very walls of the monasteries he raised — enduring emblems of a principality
  he made a **bulwark of Christianity** against the Ottomans
  [[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/029-stephen-the-great|Stephen the Great]].

The same civic memory of the founding hunt survives in the arms of neighbouring towns — the
severed **boar's head** of Roman and the **stag of St Hubert** on the seal of Baia
[[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.20 — and, above all, in the
**1701 seal of the five privileged Maramureş towns**, where two aurochs heads still stand
beside stag and arrows [[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.6.

## Why it was the natural choice for the 1858 stamp

By the eve of the Union of 1859, the aurochs head was not one symbol among many — it was
**the** symbol, five centuries old, carried by every prince from the founder to Stephen the
Great, minted on the first national coinage and cut into the seals of the state
[[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.18. When Moldavia issued its
first postage in 1858, that continuity was the point: to place the *cap de bour* on the
stamp was to make the state's most ancient voice speak in the newest medium, an assertion
of identity and legitimacy at precisely the moment Moldavia was preparing to become half of
a united Romania. This is why the "Bull's Head" issue is more than a philatelic curiosity —
it is heraldry doing patriotic work. See [[why-bull-heads-matter]] and the issue itself,
[[../issues/issue-bull-1]].
