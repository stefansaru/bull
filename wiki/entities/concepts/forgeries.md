# Forgeries of the Bull's Heads

The Moldavian *Cap de bour* ("Bull's Head") stamps of 1858 are **among the most
heavily forged classics in all of philately** — a distinction that is inseparable from
their fame and part of their intrigue. The book's own Introduction states it plainly:
"The first Moldavian stamps were heavily forged, making it difficult to identify
authentic examples. Surprisingly, even some contemporary forgeries hold significant
value, adding to their mystique"
[[../../narrative/01-introduction/002-what-makes-these-stamps-so-special|Narrative — What makes these stamps so special]].
This page consolidates who forged them, how, why they are so forgeable, and how
genuine examples are told from fakes — the missing modern **English** forgery
reference that [[../../strategy/market-research|market research]] identifies as the
book's clearest opportunity ("no consolidated modern English forgery reference despite
these being among the most-forged classics").

See also: [[scarcity-of-bull-heads]] · [[famous-collectors]] ·
[[../issues/issue-bull-1]] · [[../issues/issue-bull-2]] ·
[[../postmarks/kiriac-figures]] · [[../references/auctions/README]].

## Why these stamps are so forged

Three factors combine, and they reinforce one another.

**Fame.** They are the first Romanian stamps and a national symbol — the aurochs head,
emblem of Moldavia — issued on the eve of the union of the principalities
[[../../narrative/01-introduction/002-what-makes-these-stamps-so-special|Narrative — What makes these stamps so special]].
Fame drives demand, and demand is what a forger monetises.

**Value.** These are investment-grade rarities: examples routinely make €5k–€100k, with
record items far higher [[../../strategy/market-research|market research]]. Where a
single small piece of paper can be worth a house, the incentive to fake it — or to
"improve" a genuine but common example — is enormous.

**Crude originals that are easy to imitate yet hard to authenticate.** The 1858 stamps
were **printed by hand, one impression at a time, from single dies** onto sheets of 32,
"very irregularly placed and occasionally overlapped," so that "all values come in
distinct shades, frequently even on the same sheet" and the paper "often shows a
bluish, grayish or yellowish tint" (Scott catalogue notes, reproduced in
[[../../sources/botosani-cap-de-bour/text|Nicolau's *Cap de bour*]], pdf p.20). A crude,
variable design looks superficially trivial to copy — but the same variability makes any
single specimen hard to authenticate, because there is no crisp "correct" impression to
measure against. Authentication therefore depends on **plating** (matching an example to
the exact position it must have occupied) and on **expertising**, which is slow, costly,
and confined to a tiny pool of experts — [[../../strategy/market-research|market
research]] notes "expertisation recommended even for covers." That gap between *easy to
imitate* and *hard to authenticate* is precisely the space forgers exploit.

## The major forgers

### Costică Moroiu — the domestic forger (dies + "thousands" seized, 1889)

**Captain Constantin M. Moroiu (1839–1918)** is the emblematic *domestic* forger, and
his story is entwined with the founding of organised Romanian philately — he was a
founding member of the Romanian Philatelic Society and, in 1881, launched Romania's
first philatelic magazine, *Timbrophilo*
[[../../narrative/10-1/003-1-replica-moroiu|Narrative — 🐃 1 Replica ⏭ Moroiu]]. In that
same magazine he promoted a still-disputed claim of an earlier Moldavian issue
(handmade stamps of 15, 45, 90 and 135 parale, supposedly used 1852–1853) — values he
then produced himself
[[../../narrative/10-1/003-1-replica-moroiu|Narrative — 🐃 1 Replica ⏭ Moroiu]] [1].

Moroiu bought up printing plates of pre-1872 Romanian stamps and began producing
counterfeits. On **8 March 1889**, following a complaint by a tobacconist, the Ilfov
chief prosecutor raided the *Universul* printing house where the stamps were being made;
Wikipedia records that prosecutors "found dies for and thousands of copies of Bull's
Head stamps" [1], and a subsequent search of his home turned up "thousands of outdated
Moldavian, United Principalities, and Romanian stamps"
[[../../narrative/10-1/003-1-replica-moroiu|Narrative — 🐃 1 Replica ⏭ Moroiu]]. He was
imprisoned at Văcărești, attempted suicide, but was ultimately **acquitted for lack of
evidence** — his defence being that his customers all knew the stamps were not genuine
[[../../narrative/10-1/003-1-replica-moroiu|Narrative — 🐃 1 Replica ⏭ Moroiu]] [1].
[[../../strategy/market-research|Market research]] summarises the episode as "domestic
forger Moroiu (dies + 'thousands' seized 1889)."

### Jean de Sperati — the "master forger"

**Jean de Sperati (1884–1957)** is "one of the most infamous and skilled stamp forgers
in history," whose command of chemistry, paper, printing and philately let him produce
replicas that fooled leading experts
[[../../narrative/10-1/007-1-replica-sperati|Narrative — 🐃 1 Replica ⏭ Sperati]]. Across
his career he produced well over 500 master-quality forgeries of more than 100
issuing entities [2][4]. He assembled a *Livre d'Or* of 234 forgeries certified as
genuine by 17 different authorities in five countries, and reproduced not just the
stamp but the watermark, perforation and **cancellation**
[[../../narrative/10-1/007-1-replica-sperati|Narrative — 🐃 1 Replica ⏭ Sperati]]. He
insisted his work was *art*, calling the pieces "reproductions"; a 1942 Lisbon shipment
intercepted by French customs led to a celebrated trial in which experts first declared
the fakes genuine — the paradox that made him famous
[[../../narrative/10-1/007-1-replica-sperati|Narrative — 🐃 1 Replica ⏭ Sperati]] [2].

Sperati produced Bull's Head material, including a **108 parale** reproduction. A Sperati
die proof of the 108 parale survives, printed in black on white glazed paper, pencil-
signed, and handstamped on the reverse by the British Philatelic Association as
"Sperati Reproduction / 291" [1][2]. The book documents a Sperati 108 Parale replica
(a signed example, Corinphila 186, 2014, sold at 280 CHF) and even its **glass support
cliché** (David Feldman, 2022–2023, lot 50573, 750 CHF)
[[../../narrative/14-108-parale/012-108-parale-m1-replica-sperati|Narrative — 108 Parale
M1 Replica ⏭ Sperati]]. In 1950, as his eyesight failed, Sperati sold his archive to the
British Philatelic Association, which published the methods and diagnostics for
detecting his work
[[../../narrative/10-1/007-1-replica-sperati|Narrative — 🐃 1 Replica ⏭ Sperati]] [2].

### François Fournier — the commercial "facsimile" house

**François Fournier** ran a large commercial forgery/"facsimile" business in Geneva in
the early 20th century, marketing his products openly as inexpensive space-fillers — a
model that nonetheless flooded the market with material later passed as genuine. The
book carries a dedicated Fournier Bull's Head replica
[[../../narrative/10-1/005-1-replica-fournier|Narrative — 🐃 1 Replica ⏭ Fournier]], and
the 27 parale forgery study identifies a "Type 1 (Fournier)" reproduction alongside the
Stiedl–Billig reference types
[[../../narrative/11-27-parale/027-27-parale-replica-stiedl-billig|Narrative — 27 Parale
Replica ⏭ Stiedl-Billig]]. [[../../strategy/market-research|Market research]] lists
"Fournier, Sperati, domestic forger Moroiu" together as the forgery minefield the book
must map.

## Types of forgery

Forgery of the Bull's Heads is not one thing but several, and the more dangerous kinds
start from *genuine* material.

- **Full fakes (whole forged stamps).** Complete counterfeits printed from forgers'
  dies — Moroiu's press output, Sperati's reproductions, Fournier's facsimiles, and the
  reference "types" catalogued by Stiedl–Billig
  [[../../narrative/11-27-parale/027-27-parale-replica-stiedl-billig|Narrative — 27
  Parale Replica ⏭ Stiedl-Billig]][1][2].
- **Faked cancellations on genuine stamps / remainders.** A genuine but relatively
  common used or unused stamp is given a *rare* postmark to multiply its value. The Scott
  notes reproduced in [[../../sources/botosani-cap-de-bour/text|Nicolau]] warn directly:
  "Faked cancellations are known" and "Counterfeit cancellations exist" on the later
  Bull's Head values (pdf p.20). Nicolau records notorious fabricated strikes — the
  "grătar simplu" (simple-grid) type and a rich-clasp double-circle type — applied to
  genuine stamps *after* those postmark devices had gone out of service, which alone
  proves the cancel false (pdf p.47).
- **Faked covers and "improved" entires.** Genuine loose stamps assembled onto covers,
  or covers doctored with added strikes, to manufacture rare frankings. Nicolau even
  illustrates a genuine BOTUSCHANI-MOLDOVA 6/8 cover *paired with* a *fals* dated 28/9
  in the same (Pascanu) collection (pdf p.4). External expertising bodies report Bull's
  Head "covers" built from **1891 reprints** that have been shown to be forged for
  decades yet still resurface in auction catalogues [5].
- **Contemporary/postal fraud (fals în dauna poștei).** Distinct from collector
  forgery: 19th-century *reuse* of stamps to defraud the post. Nicolau documents fraud
  cases at the Botoșani, Bârlad and Roman offices detected by postal staff from **4
  April 1859**, in which clerks re-applied stamps and over-inked cancellations to pocket
  the fees (pdf pp.15–16, citing K. Dragomir in *Filatelia* 6/1983).

## Contemporary forgeries now have value in their own right

A striking twist: the best period forgeries have themselves become **collectable**. The
Introduction notes that "even some contemporary forgeries hold significant value"
[[../../narrative/01-introduction/002-what-makes-these-stamps-so-special|Narrative — What
makes these stamps so special]], and the Sperati narrative states his forgeries "remain
highly sought after, with some replicas valued higher than the original stamps they
imitate," now "a collecting genre in their own right"
[[../../narrative/10-1/007-1-replica-sperati|Narrative — 🐃 1 Replica ⏭ Sperati]] [2].
The market bears this out: a signed Sperati 108 parale sold for 280 CHF and his glass
cliché for 750 CHF
[[../../narrative/14-108-parale/012-108-parale-m1-replica-sperati|Narrative — 108 Parale
M1 Replica ⏭ Sperati]]. The danger is the mirror image of this value: Sperati signed
many pieces as replicas, but "many unscrupulous dealers later erased these signatures
and sold them as originals"
[[../../narrative/10-1/007-1-replica-sperati|Narrative — 🐃 1 Replica ⏭ Sperati]] [2].

## How forgeries are detected

Because the originals are so variable, detection rests on three overlapping methods.

- **Plating.** Each of the 32 positions on the sheet was a distinct hand impression, so
  a genuine stamp must correspond to a specific plate position with its characteristic
  die state, rotation and displacement; a stamp that plates to no valid position, or
  that carries features from the wrong state, is suspect
  ([[../../strategy/market-research|market research]]: "plate-by-plate treatment … die-
  state progression"). Full plate-by-plate treatment is developed under the individual
  issues [[../issues/issue-bull-1]] and [[../issues/issue-bull-2]].
- **Postmark taxonomy (cancellation authentication).** A cancel is checked against the
  documented device: its type, town, dimensions, ink colour and **period of use**. The
  public spine for this is the **Kiriac figure catalogue** — see
  [[../postmarks/kiriac-figures]] — which, for example, records that the 1858 round
  MOLDOVA datestamps used **blue / blue-green inks and never black**, and that some
  fabricated types were only introduced after the stamps had ceased to be valid, so any
  strike of them is by definition false
  [[../../sources/botosani-cap-de-bour/text|Nicolau]] pdf p.47;
  [[../postmarks/kiriac-figures]].
- **Expertising and certificates.** Because plating and postmark analysis demand rare
  expertise, high-value pieces carry expert certificates. The reference authority is
  **Fritz Heimbüchler**, whose *Die Ochsenköpfe der Moldau* is the definitive opus and
  the standard against which examples are judged
  ([[../../strategy/market-research|market research]]; Nicolau cites Heimbüchler's 1994
  census, pdf p.11). Modern Romanian practice requires an expert certificate even for
  isolated common used values before they can be accepted as genuine
  ([[../../sources/botosani-cap-de-bour/text|Nicolau]] pdf p.46). The expert pool is
  tiny, which is itself a pain point ([[../../strategy/market-research|market
  research]]).

## `debated` — the 2009 National Philatelic Museum scandal

The single most contested episode in Bull's Head authentication is the **2009
investigation of the Romanian National Philatelic Museum (Bucharest)**, which found that
holdings had been swapped for suspected forgeries. The two positions:

- **Heimbüchler's position:** the expert Fritz Heimbüchler examined museum holdings and
  **certified them forged**. A 2009 investigation reported that **215 stamps of the
  first series and 660 of the second and third** (≈ **875** pieces) had been replaced by
  suspected forgeries [1]; [[../../strategy/market-research|market research]] records the
  scandal as "~875 alleged swaps."
- **The museum's dispute:** the museum countered that **Heimbüchler had directly
  analysed only three stamps**, basing the rest of his conclusion **on scans**, and that
  authenticity therefore **remains uncertain** [1].
- **Contested valuation:** estimated losses are given as anywhere from **€3.15 million to
  €60 million** — a spread wide enough to be a flag in itself [1].

Treat this as unresolved: an expert's certification of mass forgery on one side, an
institution's methodological objection (three stamps in hand vs. scans) on the other,
and no adjudicated outcome [1] [[../../strategy/market-research|market research]].

## Practical warning

Forgeries are not a historical curiosity but a live market hazard:
[[../../strategy/market-research|market research]] records that Bull's Head forgeries are
"regularly purchased on internet auctions," and external expertising bodies confirm that
long-exposed fakes keep reappearing in sale catalogues [5]. The standing advice is to
**pre-screen** with plating and postmark taxonomy ([[../postmarks/kiriac-figures]]) and
to **expertise before buying** anything of value — including covers.
