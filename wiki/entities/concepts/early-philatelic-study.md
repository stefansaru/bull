# The study of the Bull's Heads — from Magnus 1869 to Heimbüchler

How did we come to know what we know about the Moldavian *Cap de bour* ("Bull's
Head") stamps? The knowledge was not given; it was **built**, catalogue by
catalogue, over roughly a century and a half — and the building was unusually
hard. This page traces that arc: from the first tentative listings of the 1860s,
through the long confusion over which values and issues even existed, to the
Romanian cataloguing tradition of Kiriac Dragomir and, finally, the modern German
standard of Heimbüchler. It is the story of a scholarship forced into existence by
the stamps' own defects.

## Why these stamps needed so much study

Ordinary stamps are printed from a single engraved master or a multi-subject plate,
so every copy of a value is essentially identical and the reference work is short.
The Bull's Heads are the opposite. They were struck **one impression at a time with
a hand-held die**, each Moldavian post office effectively working from its own
tooling, so that "the stamps of this emission do not present an absolute identity,
not only from one value to another, but also among stamps of the same value"
([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] p. 13). There was
**no uniform master to which a copy could be compared**. That single fact generated
three problems that every later study had to fight:

1. **Plating.** Because each value exists in several visibly different "types,"
   describing the issue means describing and ordering *all* of them. Magnus already
   set out five distinct types for each of the three long-known round values (54, 81,
   108 parale) — fifteen types in all — noting he could not guarantee the
   authenticity of every one ([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]]
   pp. 15–36). This is the ancestor of all later plate-by-plate treatment; the modern
   market-research summary lists "plating difficulty" as a headline pain point of the
   material ([[../../strategy/market-research]]).
2. **Forgery.** Precisely because genuine copies differed among themselves, "the
   margin was wide for the counterfeiters," and Magnus records that they did not fail
   to exploit "this gold-bearing seam"
   ([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] p. 6). He
   describes, for instance, a crude Hamburg forgery in which each value sits inside a
   square frame that the genuine stamps never had
   ([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] p. 40). Forgery
   remains the dominant modern research theme — see [[forgeries]].
3. **A missing documentary record.** Even the basic facts — the date of issue, the
   list of values — were contested, because the postal administration's own records
   were not to hand. Magnus notes that "one does not agree on the precise date of the
   emission" and that all of Moens's enquiries "at Jassy and at Bucharest furnished no
   other information" ([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]]
   pp. 13–14).

The consequence is that the Bull's Head literature is not a tidy catalogue line but
a **cumulative forensic project**, still not fully closed.

## The earliest listings (1861–1867): confusion over values and issues

The first catalogues barely knew the material. Magnus, writing his second edition in
1869, looks back and is blunt: "the stamps of Moldavia were for a long time rare and
very little known. It suffices to consult the first catalogues that appeared to
recognise how much this study left to be desired"
([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] p. 5).

The concrete confusion was a phantom value. The two earliest editions of
**Potiquet**'s catalogue (December 1861 and March 1862) and **Moens**'s catalogue
(1862) all listed *four rectangular* Moldavian stamps: 5 para black, 40 para blue,
**62 para green**, 80 para red ([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]]
p. 5). Magnus flags this as "a grave error": three of those belong to the later
rectangular issue, but there was never a 62-parale green stamp; the green value of the
*round* issue was a different denomination and shape entirely
([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] pp. 5–6). "These
errors were repeated on trust in the various following catalogues," until — Magnus
believes — the catalogue of **Mount Brown** carried the first correction, after which
"the unfortunate 62-para stamp disappeared from the catalogues"
([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] p. 6). This is a
clean, datable example of how a single early misreading propagated through a whole
generation of reference works before being cleared.

Two other threads of the period survive in Magnus's account. First, the **dating of
the issues** was unsettled: he cites **Rondot** (in the *Magasin Pittoresque*), who
held that stamp use began in Moldavia in 1854 during the Crimean War
([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] p. 13), and Magnus
argues explicitly against Rondot's dating of the rectangular series, placing it in
"October 1859 and not in 1858 or in 1861 as M. Rondot says"
([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] p. 40). (Modern
scholarship dates the classic round *Cap de bour* first emission to 1858 — the
persistence of these date disputes is itself part of the story.) Second, values were
still being *discovered*: Magnus reports that Moens had lately handed him a **27-para**
stamp of the first-emission type — "the first of this value that had passed under our
eyes, and which had not yet been reported by anyone" — and he devotes several pages to
arguing its authenticity, partly on the elegant ground that 27, 54, 81, 108 form an
arithmetic progression with common difference 27
([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] pp. 7, 36–39).

## Magnus 1869 as a primary artefact of the early study

Magnus's *Les timbres de Moldavie et Roumanie* (2nd edition, Brussels, at the bureau
of the journal *Le Timbre-Poste*, J.-B. Moens, 1869) is not merely a source *about*
the early study — **it is one of the earliest studies**, and reading it is reading the
knowledge being assembled in real time. Its author, "Dr. Magnus," was the pseudonym of
the French philatelist **Dr. Jacques-Amable Legrand**, a foundational figure of
scientific philately and the inventor of the perforation gauge (odontometer)
([[../../sources/scaiceanu-2011-filatelie/text|Scăiceanu 2011]]); the work was issued
through **Jean-Baptiste Moens**, whose Brussels journal *Le Timbre-Poste* ran without
interruption from 1863 to 1900 and was a principal venue of nineteenth-century
philatelic research ([[../../sources/scaiceanu-2011-filatelie/text|Scăiceanu 2011]]).

Three features mark it as pioneering method rather than a mere price list:

- **First serial plating.** The text had first appeared in *Le Timbre-Poste* from
  September 1867, where Magnus described fifteen types of the first emission; three he
  and others came to reject as doubtful
  ([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] p. 7). The 1869
  edition gives, for each of the 54, 81 and 108 para, five numbered types with
  minutely tabulated diagnostics — the set of the horns' curvature, the shape of the
  ears, the number of points on the star, the length of the post-horn in millimetres,
  the paper — a template that all later plating studies would follow
  ([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] pp. 15–36).
- **Explicit source criticism.** Magnus works from proofs "obtained directly and
  through honourable intermediaries, eliminating at the outset everything that was
  suspect," and repeatedly records the provenance of individual copies (a 54 para from
  the Foreign Office and a secretary of the late Lord Palmerston, etc.)
  ([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] pp. 6, 32). He is
  candid about uncertainty, declining to "guarantee the authenticity" of all fifteen
  types and inviting readers to send corrections
  ([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] pp. 8, 31).
- **The handstamp explanation.** Magnus already reaches the structural insight that
  the multiplicity of types arises because the postal offices "had their handstamps and
  used them as needed," which is why no two are quite alike
  ([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text|Magnus 1869]] p. 14) — the germ of
  the later postmark-and-handstamp taxonomies (see below).

## The Romanian cataloguing tradition: Kiriac Dragomir

If the nineteenth-century foundation was French and Belgian, the twentieth-century
consolidation was **Romanian**, and its central figure is **Kiriac Dragomir**, long
the acknowledged leader of the Romanian philatelic movement
([[../../sources/scaiceanu-2011-filatelie/text|Scăiceanu 2011]]). Two of his works
are load-bearing for Bull's Head study, and they carry **two different figure-numbering
systems that must not be conflated**:

1. **The 1974 reference catalogue** — *Catalogul mărcilor poștale românești*
   (Bucharest, 623 pp.), the dominant Romanian catalogue of Romanian stamps, including
   microscopic forgery-distinguishing marks. Its Chapter I is a catalogue of the classic
   1858 postmarks, and its **"Kiriac figure" numbers (Fig. 37–119)** became the *de
   facto* public reference for these cancellations, cited across auction catalogues
   ([[../references/catalogues/kiriac-1974]]). The structured derivation of that chapter — each
   figure mapped to marking type, towns, dimensions, ink and period — lives in
   [[../postmarks/kiriac-figures]].
2. **The 1990 *Ștampilografie poștală*** — Dragomir's later handstamp study, which
   **renumbers the same devices** in a different sequence (the `Fig. 271–313` range seen
   in modern auction descriptions) ([[../../strategy/market-research]]).

The trap is that a "Kiriac fig." number in an auction lot may come from *either* work,
and the two numberings do not agree. The correct concordance is by **device type plus
town**, not by figure number — e.g. the round *MOLDOVA* Iași datestamp catalogued at
Fig. 40–54 in the 1974 work corresponds to the auction "JASSY / MOLDOVA" and to
*Ștampilografie* Fig. 279 ([[../postmarks/kiriac-figures]]). This two-system problem is
one reason the postmark literature is easy to cite wrongly, and it is flagged
explicitly in the project's reference apparatus ([[../postmarks/kiriac-figures]],
[[../../strategy/market-research]]).

A third, deeper Romanian work belongs to the same tradition: **Dragomir's 2008
monograph** *Monografia … Cap de bour, em. I 1858* (291 pp.), the most detailed
Cap-de-bour-specific study, but issued in only **250 numbered copies** and in Romanian
only ([[../../strategy/market-research]]). Its scarcity and language make it
inaccessible to most collectors — a gap the modern market analysis treats as an
opportunity rather than a solved problem.

## The modern German standard: Heimbüchler

The modern definitive work is **Fritz Heimbüchler**'s *Die Ochsenköpfe der Moldau*
("The Bull's Heads of Moldavia"), a bilingual **German/English** opus published in
volumes (Band 1, 1994/95, ~418 pp.; Band 2, 2002; a third supplement, 2007), with well
over a thousand illustrations and colour plates ([[../../strategy/market-research]]).
Heimbüchler led the specialist study group **ARGE Rumänien** (founded 1969), and is
described in the Romanian literature as "the greatest expert in classic Romanian stamps
in the world" ([[../../sources/scaiceanu-2011-filatelie/text|Scăiceanu 2011]]). His work
brings the plating-and-forgery method that Magnus began to its fullest realisation:
plate-by-plate treatment of each denomination, die-state progression, and the imagery
needed to authenticate copies before paying for certificates
([[../../strategy/market-research]]).

But even the standard has limits that shape the field today: it is **out of print and
costly** (Band 1 ~€200, the set ~€300), multi-volume, and now some decades old —
bilingual rather than natively English, and predating recent finds
([[../../strategy/market-research]]). There is, as the research summary puts it, **no
modern, affordable, single-volume, comprehensive English treatment**, and no
consolidated modern English forgery reference, despite the Bull's Heads being among the
most-forged classics — the whitespace the present project aims at
([[../../strategy/market-research]]).

## The arc in one view

| Era | Work / figure | Contribution | Weakness it left |
|-----|---------------|--------------|------------------|
| 1861–62 | Potiquet; Moens (1st cat.) | First listings of the material | Phantom "62 para green"; issues confused ([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text\|Magnus 1869]] p. 5) |
| ~1862–65 | Mount Brown | First correction of the 62-para error | Still no systematic types ([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text\|Magnus 1869]] p. 6) |
| 1867–69 | **Magnus / Legrand** (via Moens, *Le Timbre-Poste*) | First systematic plating (15 types), source criticism, handstamp explanation, 27-para value | Author himself doubts several types; forgeries "very numerous" ([[../../sources/magnus-1869-timbres-moldavie/text\|Magnus 1869]] pp. 7, 40) |
| 1974 | **Kiriac Dragomir**, reference catalogue | Standard RO catalogue; "Kiriac figure" postmark numbers | Romanian-only; OOP ([[../references/catalogues/kiriac-1974]]) |
| 1990 | Kiriac Dragomir, *Ștampilografie poștală* | Fuller handstamp study | **Second, conflicting figure numbering** ([[../postmarks/kiriac-figures]]) |
| 1994–2007 | **Heimbüchler**, *Die Ochsenköpfe der Moldau* | Definitive plating + forgery reference, DE/EN | OOP, costly, multi-volume, not native EN ([[../../strategy/market-research]]) |
| 2008 | Dragomir monograph | Deepest Cap-de-bour study | 250 copies, RO only ([[../../strategy/market-research]]) |
