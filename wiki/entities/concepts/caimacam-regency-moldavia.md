# The caimacam regency of Moldavia (1856–1859)

When the [[../places/atelia-timbrului|Stamp Workshop]] in Iași struck Moldavia's
first postage stamps in the summer of 1858, Moldavia had **no reigning prince**.
It was governed by a *caimacam* — an Ottoman-appointed regent, a lieutenant
standing in for an absent throne — and the postal reform that called those stamps
into being was decreed in that regent's name. The four **Bull's Head** values are,
quite literally, an artifact of the caimacam regime. This page explains what that
regime was, who ran it, and why the men who signed Moldavia into its own first
stamps were, in the very years they did so, mostly working *against* the national
cause those stamps have come to symbolise. For the wider frame see
[[geopolitics-1800s]].

> [!note] A source with a thesis
> The single documentary source used here — *Documente privind Unirea
> Principatelor*, vol. II (1959) — is an Austrian-consular archive edited in
> Communist-era Romania. Its editors write with an explicit thesis: they call the
> caimacams' conduct **"deseori trădătoare"** (often *treacherous*) and the great
> boyars **"reacţionară"** (reactionary) ([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.10).
> Those are **the source's characterisations**, quoted as such below and not
> adopted here as neutral fact; the political leanings they describe are
> well documented, but the moral verdict is a period framing (cf. DR-006).

## What a *caimacam* was

A *caimacam* (Ottoman *kaymakam*) was a **provisional regent** appointed to hold a
principality between reigning princes. In Moldavia the office came into force after
the last reigning prince, **Grigore Alexandru Ghica**, left in 1856 at the close of
his term. The 1959 editors open their story precisely here, listing among the
volume's revelations **"înlocuirea lui Grigore Ghica prin Teodor Balş"** — the
replacement of Ghica by Teodor Balș — and note that Ghica, who in his last months
"worked in close connection with the unionist movement," had tried to hand power to
a *unionist* "caimacamia of three," a formula "at which one would not arrive until
1858" ([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] pp.10, 20).
That attempt failed; instead the Porte installed a single regent hostile to union.

Because the regent held office at the Sultan's pleasure and under the eye of the
occupying Habsburg power, the caimacam period was one of maximal foreign leverage.
The source's summary is blunt: the Austrian representative "keeps the caimacam under
his surveillance — Balș and then Vogoride — imposing on him the **anti-unionist**
line of conduct, criticising his every scruple or hesitation"
([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.11).

## Teodor Balș, caimacam 1856–1857

**Teodor (Toderiță) Balș** took over Moldavia in mid-1856. The Austrian consul
**Godel-Lannoy** greeted "the passing of power into the hands of Toderiță Balș" with
satisfaction and became, in the editors' phrase, the **"eminenţa cenuşie"** — the
grey eminence — and all-powerful hand behind the "new reactionary and anti-unionist
regime" ([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.20). On
16 July 1856 the consul could report a meeting with Balș and General Gablenz,
commander of the Austrian occupation corps, to fix the list of a new government of
"determined enemies of the Union"
([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] pp.20–21).

Balș's subservience showed at once in economic policy. With his installation Austria
moved to cancel the concessions Ghica had granted foreign (Prussian and French)
companies; Balș "had promised — on the very day of his investiture — to submit to the
Porte" the privileges of the Bank of Moldavia and of the French captain Magnan, and
the consul soon reported the Magnan concession annulled, taking it as "a real proof
of his inclinations" ([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] pp.24–25).
Under him the general policy of the state, and "especially the **'separatist'
policy** of the two caimacams (Balș and then Vogoride)," ran along lines the consul
"directed" ([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.20).

Balș fell ill and, on **1 March 1857**, Godel-Lannoy announced his death
([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.35).

## Nicolae Vogoride, caimacam 1857 — and the falsified elections

After a short interregnum the office passed to **Nicolae Conachi-Vogoride**,
son-in-law of the poet Costache Conachi. Balș had recommended him to the Porte, days
before dying, as a man fit "to continue the policy traced by the Sublime [Porte]";
Vogoride "from the first days went over to the separatist camp, continuing with fury
the treacherous policy of his predecessor," assuring Austria of "the same devotion as
his predecessor" ([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.35).
The editors reserve their sharpest language for him: they speak of **"'Devotamentul'
celor doi caimacami faţă de Poartă şi Austria"** — the "devotion" of the two
caimacams toward the Porte and Austria — "and their betrayal of the interests of the
Romanian people," and record that his apologies to Austria in the Csallner affair (an
Austrian soldier roughed up by a Moldavian officer) "reach the summit of servility"
([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.11).

Vogoride's regency is remembered above all for the **falsification of the July 1857
elections** to the [[../events/ad-hoc-divans-1857|ad-hoc Divan]]. The Divan had been
mandated by the Treaty of Paris to voice Moldavia's wishes on union; a genuine vote
was certain to be unionist, so the regent and his Austrian backers manipulated the
electoral lists. A consular report of **20 May 1857** openly admits the interference
— the "gross falsification of the electoral lists" checked "with care by the Minister
of the Interior with the help of trustworthy persons"
([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.37) — and the
consul defended Vogoride, "his accomplice," against the charges
([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.37).

The scandal broke when the unionists **seized and published part of Vogoride's secret
correspondence**. The consul himself described how "their secret agents penetrated
right into the cabinet of the caimacam, and knew how to procure from there copies of
several papers, alongside an original letter from his brother at the Ottoman embassy
in London" ([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.37) —
letters that laid the rigging bare. Encouraged by a telegram from Vienna's foreign
minister Buol urging "resistance," Vogoride at first "rejected the protest of the four
powers, refusing to accept the annulment of the elections"
([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.37). But the
matter was decided over his head, at European level: the rupture of the four powers'
relations with the Porte and the **Osborne interview** — where Napoleon III gave up
full union but "obtained the annulment of the elections" — forced the vote's
cancellation ([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.38).
The re-run elections of autumn 1857 returned an overwhelming unionist Divan. The
editors mark this as the turning point: "the annulment of the falsified elections of
July 1857 represents the beginning of the retreat" of Austrian influence
([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.11).

## The *căimăcămia de trei*, 1858–1859

The **Convention of Paris (7/19 August 1858)** — see [[../events/paris-convention-1858]]
— replaced the single regent with a three-man regency, the **"caimacamia of three"
(*căimăcămia de trei*)**. As the editors note, the Convention "provided for the
replacement of the caimacams and, indirectly, ensured the success of the cause of the
Union"; in Moldavia, thanks to precautions taken by the unionist leadership in the
last part of Ghica's reign, "the caimacamia of three was to be dominated by
unionists" ([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.40).
With Vogoride's removal the picture reversed completely: "With the removal of
Vogoride, the effective action of Austrian interference found its end in Moldavia,"
and the source contrasts him with his successors, who "will no longer send to Vienna
lines full of humility" ([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] p.41).

It was this **pro-unionist majority** of the three-man regency that organised the
**elective Assembly** which, on **5/17 January 1859**, chose **Alexandru Ioan Cuza**
— see [[../events/union-of-principalities-1859]]. The change of political weather is
audible even in the hostile consul's dispatches: after "the taking of authority by
the caimacamia of three, in majority unionist," he complained of its work, and when
it came to "the elections watched over by the majority of the caimacamia of three,
known for its unionism," he could only accuse the caimacams of "arbitrary" conduct —
the very charge his own side had earned in 1857
([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea docs]] pp.43–44).

## The Bull's Head link

This is the political fact that makes the caimacam regency inseparable from the
stamps. Moldavia's 1858 postal reform — the **"Project for the Reform of the Posts"**
— was, in the archival heading, "drawn up by the commission appointed by **His
Excellency the Prince-Caimacam** and approved by the **Administrative Council of
Moldavia**, to take effect from 1 May 1858"
([[../../narrative/04-1858/017-1858-postal-reform-iaşi-jassy|Postal Reform 1858]];
[[postal-history-romania]]). The first Moldavian postage stamps were "planned in
accordance with the norms established by this reform"
([[../../narrative/04-1858/017-1858-postal-reform-iaşi-jassy|Postal Reform 1858]]).

Read against the timeline above, the paradox is sharp. The reform decreed in the
Prince-Caimacam's name took effect on 1 May 1858, and the [[../issues/issue-bull-1|first
Bull's Heads]] were issued that July — during the **hand-over** from Vogoride's
separatist regency to the unionist *căimăcămia de trei* installed under the August
1858 Convention. So Moldavia's own ancient aurochs arms went onto its own postage
under a government that had, months earlier, been rigging elections to keep the
country *out* of union — and were used through the last months of separate Moldavia,
right up to the January 1859 election that erased the separate state the caimacams had
been appointed to hold. The stamps are the postal signature of a Moldavia with no
reigning prince: an artifact of the regency itself.
