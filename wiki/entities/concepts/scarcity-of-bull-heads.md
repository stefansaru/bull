# The scarcity of the Bull's Heads

Almost everything that makes the Moldavian *Cap de bour* famous flows from a single
fact: **there are barely enough of them to go around.** The stamps are crude to look at
— "simple designs, unclear text, and even the paper quality is often poor"
([[../../narrative/01-introduction/002-what-makes-these-stamps-so-special|what makes these stamps special]]) —
yet an authentic example trades in a band of **€5,000–€100,000**, and the finest items
reach six and even seven figures [1][3]. That premium is scarcity made visible. This page
sets out **how few survive, why so few survive, how the rarity is distributed across the
values, and what the stamps have been worth from the 19th century to today's records.**

## How many survive? — a `debated` figure, not a single number

The total number of surviving first-issue Bull's Heads is **genuinely disputed**, and the
sources do not agree. The honest answer is a **range of roughly 690 to "under 800,"**
depending on whose census you use and whether museum-held and long-lost pieces are
counted. Each figure below traces to a specific inventory:

| Figure | Source / census | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **~630** | 1983 Munich inventory (K.D., *Almanahul Filatelic 1983*) | "630 marci Cap de bour" then known in collections [[../../sources/botosani-cap-de-bour/text\|Botoșani monograph]] |
| **687** | Jean-Pierre Mangin inventory, 1994 (*Filatelia 7–8/94*); reported by Wikipedia as a "1994 study" | 95 unused + 592 used across the four values [1][[../../sources/botosani-cap-de-bour/text\|Botoșani monograph]] |
| **714** | Feldman "Classic Romania" census, built on Heimbüchler | 524 recorded + 145 not seen since 1950 + 45 in institutions = 714 [3] |
| **724 / 750** | Intermediate figures in circulation | Cited among the conflicting counts in [[../../strategy/market-research\|market research]] |
| **"fewer than 800" / "over 800"** | Feldman "Classic Romania" summary estimate | "fewer than 800 in total of all four values now exist, with some 100 of these on covers" [3] |

The spread is not carelessness — it reflects **what each counter includes.** The 714 and
"under 800" figures fold in pieces "locked away in museums world-wide" (the Tapling
Collection at the British Library, the German, Bavarian, Swedish and Romanian postal
museums) and items "not seen since 1950" — the King Carol II collection, and the von
Witzleben collection taken to the Soviet Union in 1945 [3][[../../sources/botosani-cap-de-bour/text|Botoșani monograph]].
A stricter census of what has actually surfaced at auction lands lower, nearer Mangin's
687. **The book should present this as a `debated` range, attributing each number, and not
pretend to a false precision** ([[../../strategy/market-research|market research]]).

For the *usage-level* granularity behind these totals — which towns, which cancels, how
many on cover — see the [[../postmarks/census|auction postmark census]] and the
[[../references/bulls-head-lots|notable auction lots]].

## Why so few survive

**1. They were barely printed at all.** The first issue was tiny from the start. Only
**24,064 stamps** were printed across all four values — 6,016 of the 27 parale, 10,016 of
the 54 parale, just **2,016 of the 81 parale**, and 6,016 of the 108 parale [1]. Compare
this to the millions struck for ordinary definitives elsewhere; the Bull's Heads were a
provincial, short-lived emergency issue for [[../issues/issue-bull-1|Moldavia's first
stamps]], sold only from July to October 1858.

**2. More than half were destroyed unsold.** Of the 24,064 delivered to post offices,
only about **11,746 were ever sold** before the issue was withdrawn; the remaining
**~12,308–12,318 were officially destroyed** [1][3][[../../sources/botosani-cap-de-bour/text|Botoșani monograph]].
Sources disagree on the mechanism — the Botoșani monograph and Feldman describe unsold
remainders being formally destroyed (Feldman notes 12,308 of the 108-parale-era stock
returned and destroyed), while Wikipedia attributes the loss to an **1874 fire in
Bucharest** [1]. Either way, the practical effect is the same: **the surviving population
was more than halved before it ever left the post office.**

**3. The paper is crude and fragile.** These were printed one impression at a time on
thin, poorly made horizontally-laid and wove papers, often with a bluish or lilac tinge,
using badly-mixed ink that ran "watery" and blotchy [3][[../../sources/botosani-cap-de-bour/text|Botoșani monograph]].
Creases, thins and paper irregularities are so common that on the auction market they are
described as "the rule," and paper pinholes are routinely mistaken for damage
([[../../strategy/market-research|market research]]). Sound, full-margined examples are a
small fraction of even the small surviving total — lot after lot in the
[[../references/bulls-head-lots|auction record]] is caveated "thinned," "creased,"
"touched," "backed."

**4. Ordinary postal use destroyed most of them.** Like all early stamps, the great
majority were used on everyday letters and newspapers that were simply thrown away.
Survival depended on the accident of a **correspondence being kept** — Feldman notes that
the ~100 surviving covers are "the fortuitous result of two correspondences having
survived" [3]. Stamps used on newspapers and printed matter fared worst of all, since
that class of mail was almost never preserved.

**5. Heavy forgery thinned the genuine stock's visibility.** The Bull's Heads are among
the most-forged classics in the world — Fournier, Sperati and the domestic forger Moroiu
all attacked them, and forgeries are "regularly purchased on internet auctions"
([[../../strategy/market-research|market research]]). Forgery does not reduce the number
of genuine stamps, but it does two things to scarcity: it makes **authentication
essential** (nearly every serious item carries a Heimbüchler or Diena certificate — see
the certs threaded through [[../references/bulls-head-lots|the lots]]), and it means many
"Bull's Heads" in old collections are worthless, so the pool of *certifiably genuine*
material is smaller than the raw survivor count suggests. See [[forgeries]].

**6. Wars and thefts removed whole collections.** Feldman estimates that **"about one
third to one quarter of all recorded items have disappeared from the marketplace"** [3].
The von Witzleben collection was carried to the USSR in 1945 (now in the St. Petersburg
postal museum); King Carol II's collection, pledged as loan collateral and forfeited,
vanished; and a group of items destined for a 1988 London auction was stolen at the
airport and never reappeared [3][[../../sources/botosani-cap-de-bour/text|Botoșani monograph]].
These pieces are still *counted* in the census but are effectively unobtainable — which is
why the "in private hands, available" number is lower than any headline figure. See
[[famous-collectors]].

## Rarity by value — and the rarest of all

Within the first issue the survival totals are strikingly uneven (Feldman / Heimbüchler
census [3]):

| Value | Printed | Recorded surviving | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| 27 parale (black/rose) | 6,016 | ~174 (Feldman) / 176 (1994) | The "common" value — still rare in absolute terms |
| 54 parale (blue/green) | 10,016 | ~283 (Feldman) / 262 (1994) | Most printed, most survivors |
| **81 parale (blue/blue)** | **2,016** | **~62** | **The supreme rarity of the first issue** |
| 108 parale (blue/rose) | 6,016 | ~195 (Feldman) / 187 (1994) | Rare; ~30 covers/fronts recorded |

**The 81 parale is the rarest and most coveted.** Only 2,016 were ever printed, and only
about **62 survive** in any form (roughly 42 recorded + 12 unseen since 1950 + 8 in
museums) [3][1]. Feldman calls the "GALATZ" used example "the rarest of all the Bull's
Heads," noting that of the recorded pieces four have not been seen since 1950 and three
more are in museums [[../references/bulls-head-lots|(lot)]]. Of the 27 recorded *used*
examples, **exactly one is cancelled in red** — the unique Jassy strike — and just about
seven are known from Fokschani [[../references/bulls-head-lots|(lots)]]. In **mint,
never-hinged, original-gum condition only two 81-parale copies are believed to survive,**
valued at **€70,000–€100,000** [1].

**Rarity within rarity — cancels and covers.** Beyond the value, scarcity is driven by
*where* a stamp was used. Small offices received "only 5,000 or fewer stamps over the
life of the issue," so a clear strike from a minor town is far rarer than the value alone
[3]. The auction record bristles with such rarities: only **three** 54-parale known from
Foltitscheni; only **19–20** 27-parale from Fokschani; the **unique** Dorohoj 27-parale
cover; a **single** recorded 108-parale cover from Piatra
[[../references/bulls-head-lots|(lots)]]. The **greatest single item of all** — franked
with the 27, 81 and two 108 parale together — is held in the German Postal Museum and
will never come to market [3].

**The second issue's hidden supreme rarity.** In the [[../issues/issue-bull-2|1858–61
second issue]] the 40 and 80 parale are commoner, but the **5 parale "PORTO GAZETEI" Type
I** is, in Feldman's words, "the supreme rarity of the Moldavia Bulls": of 23,040 printed
on bluish paper, **only 3 unused and 32 used survive** [3]. Heimbüchler records only ~8
unused examples across papers [[../references/bulls-head-lots|(lots)]]. The famous **"Large
Journal"** — a single *Zimbrul și Vulturul* newspaper wrapper franked with **eight** of
the 5-parale — accounts for **one quarter of all known used examples of the stamp on its
own** [3].

## What they have been worth — then and now

Scarcity has commanded high prices for well over a century. The Botoșani monograph's
price tables show the 27 parale already catalogued in the **tens of thousands of francs by
the 1940s–1960s**, and the "Large Journal" sold at Köhler in **1928 for 56,000 RM against
a 10,000 RM estimate** — a stunning multiple even then [2][[../../sources/botosani-cap-de-bour/text|Botoșani monograph]].

Modern results (all genuine, certified pieces):

- **General band:** an authentic Bull's Head is worth **€5,000–€100,000** depending on
  value, condition and cancel [1][3].
- **81 parale, unused, ex-Ferrary:** **€42,700** at David Feldman, December 2019 [2].
- **81 parale, used, unique red cancel:** **$50,132** at Corinphila, 2013 (catalogued
  Mi €48,000 / Scott $55,000) [4][[../references/bulls-head-lots|(lot)]].
- **27 parale on cover:** **€26,000** at Artmark (Bucharest) in 2015 — then the most
  expensive stamp ever sold at auction in Romania [6].
- **108 parale, unused:** estimated **€10,000–€15,000**; Michel values reach ~€32,000
  [4].
- **The "Large Journal" (5 parale × 8 on wrapper):** the record item of Romanian
  philately — sold in **2006/2007 for "just over US$1,000,000" per Feldman**, reported
  elsewhere as **~€750,000** [2][1]. The [[../../strategy/market-research|market research]]
  places it in a **~€750k–830k** band; the exact headline is itself `debated`.

## What scarcity means for the stamps' fame

The Bull's Heads are not famous because they are beautiful — they are famous because they
are **almost impossibly rare, historically loaded, and treacherously easy to fake.** The
tiny print run and the destruction of remainders capped the population before collecting
even began; crude paper, postal attrition, wars and thefts whittled it further; and the
survivors are so unevenly distributed that a whole sub-hobby of *plating and cancel-hunting*
exists to chase them. That combination is exactly why the serious collector base is
"low hundreds, not thousands" yet the money is real ([[../../strategy/market-research|market
research]]) — scarcity concentrates demand onto a handful of objects and turns each into a
trophy. It is also why **authentication and census work are the discipline's centre of
gravity**: when only ~62 of a stamp exist, knowing precisely which 62 — and telling them
from the forgeries — *is* the subject. See [[bull-heads-today]] for the current market and
[[forgeries]] for the authentication problem.
