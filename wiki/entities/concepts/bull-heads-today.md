# The Bull's Heads today

> This page closes the arc that begins in 1858. It asks what the Moldavian
> *Cap de bour* **is now** — what a genuine example is worth on today's market,
> which surviving items are the crown jewels, where a member of the public can
> actually see one, who still collects and studies them, and how the bull's head
> lives on as a state symbol. It keeps documented fact separate from present-day
> status, and ties back to [[why-bull-heads-matter]]. For the deeper background
> see [[scarcity-of-bull-heads]], [[famous-collectors]] and the
> [[../../strategy/market-research|market research]].

## What a Bull's Head is worth now

Nearly a century and a half after they were withdrawn, the Bull's Heads are
**investment-grade classics.** A genuine, certified example trades in a broad band
of roughly **€5,000 to €100,000**, with the finest and rarest pieces reaching well
into six figures ([[scarcity-of-bull-heads]]; [[../../strategy/market-research|market
research]]) [2][4]. The price is a direct function of scarcity: fewer than 800 items
of all four values are thought to survive, so demand from a small, expert market
concentrates onto a handful of trophy objects ([[scarcity-of-bull-heads]]).

Recent auction results give the band its shape. Among the documented modern sales
([[../../strategy/market-research|market research]]):

- **81 parale, used, unique red cancel** — **$50,132** at Corinphila (2013),
  catalogued around Mi €48,000 [4].
- **81 parale, unused, ex-Ferrary** — **€42,700** at David Feldman (December 2019)
  [3].
- **108 parale, unused** — **$34,217** (2018); a fine used 54 parale reached about
  **€30,000 / $34,000** at Feldman [12].
- **A group of three unused heads** — about **€92,000** (2015).
- **27 parale on cover** — **€26,000** at Artmark in Bucharest (2015), reported at
  the time as the most expensive stamp ever sold at auction in Romania [13].

The stamps also move as major named collections come apart. In **December 2022**
David Feldman dispersed the **Hausman collection**, which included eight Bull's Head
examples, keeping the material in front of the market's low-hundreds core
([[famous-collectors]]) [5]. The catalogue values themselves are widely regarded as
inadequate to real trading levels, and the census behind them remains a `debated`
range — reasons the field leans so heavily on provenance and expertisation
([[../../strategy/market-research|market research]]).

## The most famous surviving item: the "Large Journal"

The single most celebrated survivor is not a stamp at all but a **newspaper.** A
complete issue of the Iași paper *Zimbrulu și Vulturulu* ("The Aurochs and the
Eagle"), dated **11 November 1858**, was franked with **eight** of the second issue's
5 parale "PORTO GAZETEI" stamps — on its own accounting for about a quarter of all
known used examples of that supreme rarity ([[scarcity-of-bull-heads]]) [1]. Now
immortalised as the **"Large Journal,"** it passed through Maurice Burrus and Georges
Tomasini before reaching the block ([[famous-collectors]]).

On **3 December 2006** David Feldman sold it in Geneva. The hammer was **€700,000**
(about US$927,000 at the time), and with fees and commission the total reached
**€829,500** — over US$1.1 million — bought by the London-based collector **Joseph
Hackmey.** It is described as the most expensive item of Romanian philately and,
more broadly, the **highest price ever paid for a single newspaper** [1][3]. Feldman
reports the all-in figure as "just over US$1,000,000"; the exact headline is itself
`debated`, and the [[../../strategy/market-research|market research]] places it in a
**~€750k–830k** band [3][1]. Either way it is the record that anchors the whole field.

## Where the public can see them

Because so much of the surviving stock is institutional, some of the best Bull's
Heads are, in Feldman's phrase, "locked away in museums world-wide" — counted in the
census but permanently off the market ([[scarcity-of-bull-heads]]) [4]:

- **The National Museum of Romanian History (MNIR), Bucharest.** Housed in the former
  **Palatul Poștelor** on Calea Victoriei — the very Post Palace the service built and
  occupied in 1899–1900 — the museum's **Philatelic Collection holds all the original
  dies** of the 1858 Bull's Head issues, together with an unissued die, lithographic
  stones and postal stationery documenting the manufacturing stages
  ([[../places/old-post-buildings]]). There is currently **no permanent philatelic
  exhibition** — the collection is largely in store during an inventory, and the
  building itself is under a major multi-year restoration — but the dies are the
  physical origin of every genuine stamp ([[../places/old-post-buildings]]).
- **The British Library, London — the Tapling Collection.** Bull's Head examples sit
  among the world-class classics donated from the estate of **Thomas Tapling in 1891**,
  now held in the British Library's Philatelic Collections [10][4].
- **Continental postal museums.** Institutional holdings include the German and
  Bavarian postal museums (the German Postal Museum holds the greatest single item of
  all — a cover franked with the 27, 81 and two 108 parale together), the Swedish
  postal museum, and the St. Petersburg postal museum, which received the von Witzleben
  collection carried east in 1945 ([[scarcity-of-bull-heads]]; [[famous-collectors]])
  [4].

The rarities also surface, briefly and under glass, at major exhibitions — see EFIRO
below.

## The collecting community today

The serious global base is **low hundreds, not thousands**, and it is organised around
a handful of institutions ([[../../strategy/market-research|market research]]):

- **Societies.** The **ARGE Rumänien** study group (German/Swiss, est. 1969) is the
  hard core — just **27 active members from eight countries in January 2025**, a figure
  that understates but frames the field. The **Society for Romanian Philately** (US)
  publishes *Romanian Philatelic Studies* in English
  ([[../../strategy/market-research|market research]]).
- **The state issuer and federation.** **ROMFILATELIA**, Romania's state philatelic
  agency, keeps the *Cap de bour* in the public eye through anniversary programmes — it
  marked the **160th anniversary** of the first issue with a *Romanian Postage Stamp
  Day – Philatelic Premieres* release, noting the stamp was printed on 15 July 1858 and
  put in circulation on 22 July 1858 [11].
- **EFIRO.** The Romanian Philatelic Federation stages **EFIRO**, the country's FIP
  world exhibition. **EFIRO 2024** (Bucharest, 16–19 April 2024) was the **only FIP
  World Stamp Exhibition of 2024**, held at the National Library of Romania with over
  **560 exhibits from more than 400 participants across 67 countries** on some 2,560
  frames — a stage on which Romanian classics and rarities from private collections and
  museums are shown [6][7].
- **Dealers and auction houses.** The high-value trade runs through the specialist
  houses — **David Feldman, Corinphila, Heinrich Köhler, Spink** — and specialist
  literature dealers, with authentication by **Heimbüchler** (and historically Diena and
  others) as the connective tissue of every strong lot
  ([[famous-collectors]]; [[../../strategy/market-research|market research]]).

## The aurochs as a living national symbol

The animal on the 1858 stamps never stopped being a symbol of statehood. The
**aurochs's (bull's / *zimbru*) head with a star between its horns** was the historic
arms of the Principality of Moldavia, and it survives today on the **coat of arms of
the Republic of Moldova**: an aurochs head bearing an eight-pointed star, flanked by a
five-petalled rose and a crescent, borne on the breast of an eagle [8][9]. The
Presidency of Moldova describes it as "the recognised emblem belonging to the land of
Moldavia, perpetuated over the centuries as the official sign of the country" [9]. The
old founding legend — Prince Dragoș hunting an aurochs, his hound *Molda* giving her
name to the river and then the land — still travels with the emblem [8]. So the same
image that a Moldavian clerk pressed onto a crude scrap of laid paper in 1858 is,
today, on the passport, the seal and the flag-adjacent arms of a modern European
state. (Whether the beast is properly an *aurochs* or a *zimbru*/bison is itself
genuinely contested — see [[aurochs]] and the [[../../strategy/market-research|market
research]].)

## Why they still matter

The Bull's Heads endure for the reasons this whole story has been building toward.
They are **almost impossibly rare** — a few hundred survivors, unevenly spread across
four values, with the 81 parale and the 5 parale "Large Journal" as the near-mythic
peaks ([[scarcity-of-bull-heads]]). They are **historically loaded** — Moldavia's first
stamps, an emblem of a principality feeling its way toward the union that became
Romania, and the model that Greece, Turkey, Serbia and others soon followed [11]. And
they are **treacherous** — among the most-forged classics ever made, which turns
authentication and census work into the discipline's true subject
([[scarcity-of-bull-heads]]).

That is why the present-day picture looks the way it does: seven-figure records for a
newspaper, dies guarded in a former post palace turned national museum, a study group of
twenty-seven that still convenes, and a bull's head that has jumped from postage to
national arms. The crude little stamp outlived the post office that made it, the kingdom
that treasured it, and most of the collectors who chased it — and it is still, today, one
of the most coveted objects in philately. See [[why-bull-heads-matter]] for why that was
never really about the paper.
