# Foreign post offices in the Principalities

When Moldavia issued its *Cap de bour* stamps in July 1858, it was not the only
post working the roads of the country. On the same soil, and often through the
same relay horses, ran the **postal services of foreign Great Powers** — above
all the **Austrian** (called in Romanian *poșta nemțească*, "the German post")
and the **Russian**. They pre-dated the national reform by decades, they carried
much of the country's mail to the outside world, and they survived the
[[../events/union-of-principalities-1859|Union of 1859]] by a full ten years. The
fragile state post that the Bull's Heads embodied had, quite literally, to fight
for its own letters on its own territory. This page follows those rival offices;
for the national reform they competed with, see [[postal-history-romania]] §6,
which this page deliberately goes beyond.

## Two posts in the capitals

The foreign services were physical offices, not abstractions. In **1830** a
**Russian postal office** already operated in Bucharest, with despatches to
Craiova and Galați; by **1840** a similar Russian office was working in Iași as
well ([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.160). The
Bucharest Russian office ran ordinary couriers toward Galați and extraordinary
couriers on the long routes Bucharest–Constantinople, Bucharest–Odessa, and
Bucharest through Iași to St Petersburg
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.160). At about
the same time „austriacii înființară și ei oficii poștale pe lângă consulatele
din București și Iași, și mai târziu și în alte orașe" — the Austrians set up
their own offices beside their consulates in Bucharest and Iași, and later in
other towns, forwarding correspondence in sealed pouches (*gente închise*)
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.160).

Minescu locates them precisely in Bucharest. The **Austrian or German post**
(*Poșta Austriacă sau Nemțească*) had its seat „lângă colegiul și biserica Sf.
Sava" — by the Sf. Sava college and church, on the ground between the statue of
Michael the Brave and strada Academiei. The **Russian post** stood nearby, „pe
lângă strada Doamnei," between strada Academiei and Coltea
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.164). A period
almanac even lists their timetables next to the Romanian post's, and notes that
the Austrian service „aduce și gazetele străine" — it also brought in the foreign
newspapers, running twice a week for the states of Austria, Germany, Italy,
France and England
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.164). So
although the offices actually planted on Romanian soil were the Austrian and
Russian ones, it was through the Austrian channel that mail for **France,
England and the rest of western Europe** left the country.

## Legal basis — and a standing disagreement

The foreign offices rested on a claim of right that Bucharest never accepted. The
interested Powers held that „postele lor funcționează în România în baza
tratatelor încheiate cu Turcia" — that their posts functioned in Romania by
virtue of the **treaties concluded with the Ottoman Porte**; since the
Principalities lay under Ottoman suzerainty, those treaties bound them too
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.228). The
Romanian government replied, „cu drept cuvânt" (with good reason, in Minescu's
phrase), that the **Treaty and Convention of Paris of 1856 and 1858** had
provided that the Romanian lands should administer themselves, free of outside
interference ([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]]
p.228; for the wider settlement see [[geopolitics-1800s]] and
[[../events/crimean-war-principalities]]).

> **Debate (DR-006).** The source records a genuine legal contest, not a settled
> point: Ottoman-treaty right (the Powers) versus Paris-guaranteed
> self-administration (Romania). Minescu takes the Romanian side, but presents
> both positions; the wiki leaves it as a disagreement, unresolved by the parties
> until the offices were physically withdrawn.

## How they undercut the national post

The rivalry was never on equal terms, because the national post was made to serve
its own competitors. Under the road contracts — the 1841 Wallachian contract is
quoted at length — the Romanian contractors (*contraccii*) were bound to receive
the Austrian K.K. agency's sealed pouch and forward it under dedicated couriers,
and to give Russian couriers fresh carts and horses „fără cea mai mică
împiedicare și zăbavă," without the least hindrance or delay
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.160). The
transport of these foreign couriers „se făcea prin căruțele și caii poștelor
românești" — was carried on the carts and horses of the Romanian posts, whose
contractors had to give every assistance in return for fees no higher than a
private person paid ([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei
1916]] p.160). The national service thus supplied the muscle while the foreign
office kept the customer.

Worse, a later abuse turned the Romanian post into a free carrier for its rival.
Director **Fălcoianu**'s report of 1867 documents that international
correspondence „se purtau gratis mai în toată țara prin poșta română" — was
carried free across most of the country by the Romanian post, smuggled inside
the diplomatic bags as if it were official mail
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.273). Inspection
of the Austrian post's official pouches „s-au dovedit corespondențe
internaționale private, ziare străine, scrisori și chiar gropuri de bani" —
turned up private international letters, foreign newspapers, letters and even
parcels of money ([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]]
p.273). The Romanian post carried this traffic for nothing while „poșta austriacă
prelevează taxe pentru acea corespondență" — the Austrian post charged full rates
for it ([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.273).

## Expansion after 1859

The Union did not dislodge them. In the period from **1859** to the eventual
merger of posts and telegraphs, „serviciul postal austriac și rusesc continuă de
a funcționa în țara noastră ca și în trecut" — the Austrian and Russian services
went on operating exactly as before; and the Austrian one, Minescu writes,
„a și progresat" — actually grew, „prin înmulțirea numărului de biurouri poștale
și prin atragerea în cercul lor de acțiune a întregului schimb de corespondențe
și mesagerii cu străinătatea": multiplying its offices and drawing to itself
almost the **whole exchange of foreign correspondence and parcels**
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.228). The very
success of the young national post at home was matched by its exclusion from the
international traffic.

## The long campaign to expel them, 1861–1868

From **1861** onward, successive Romanian governments made representations to
Austria and Russia for the suppression of their posts and the transfer of the
service to the Romanian post — „nu s-a putut însă ajunge la nici un rezultat,"
without reaching any result
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.228). The
Fălcoianu report confirms the same long stalemate: the government had tried „încă
din anul 1861 a face să înceteze poșta străină," but everything reduced to
exchanges of notes to which the Austrian and Russian agents replied only after
three or four months; in **1865** Austria at last allowed a Romanian delegate to
come to Vienna to negotiate a postal convention, „dară rezultatul a fost nul, ca
totdeauna" — but the result was null, as always
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.271).

### The Ghica "calculus" retort (recorded anecdote)

The most famous episode belongs to the statesman **Ion Ghica**, who around
**1863** was foreign minister. One objection the Powers raised was that Romanian
personnel could not run such a service. Minescu quotes Ghica's own account (from
his *Scrisori către Alexandri*, cited as p.25): a consul asked him where Romania
had the „oameni speciali, capabili și experimentați" to be entrusted with
diplomatic correspondence, and Ghica answered that a people who „am putut învăța
calculul diferențial și integral" — had been able to learn the **differential and
integral calculus**, handling functions as well as Delaunay and Puiseux — could
certainly, in a few months if not a few weeks, learn „a manipula primirea,
expedierea și distribuirea scrisorilor și pachetelor," to receive, despatch and
sort letters and parcels as well as the Russian and Austrian postal clerks did
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.228).

> **Anecdote (C-17).** This is a recorded first-person anecdote, attributed by
> Minescu to Ion Ghica's published letters to Vasile Alecsandri, not an
> independently documented exchange. It is reproduced here for its color and as
> evidence of the Romanian argument, and is flagged as such per the legend/fact
> rule.

Ghica himself concedes the real reason nothing moved: Romania could not „susține
lupta cu cele două mari Puteri" — sustain a fight with two Great Powers on whom
larger questions of national existence depended, so „chestiunea poștelor se
tărăgăna și amâna din zi în zi," the postal question was dragged out and put off
from day to day ([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]]
p.228).

## The wind-down: 31 March 1869

The knot was finally cut by convention rather than confrontation. Once the last
formalities with Austria were completed, it was agreed „de comun acord" that on
the **evening of 31 March 1869 (New Style)** the Austrian postal service should
„înceteze orice operațiuni în țara noastră" — cease all operations in the country
— and that from **1 April 1869 (New Style)** the Romanian post would itself
handle the international service
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.281). The public
inauguration was staged with ceremony: a contemporary notice in the *Revista
Telegraf și Poșta* dated **20 March (Old Style)** — the same 1 April New Style —
announced that „astăzi… s-au desființat poștele străine în România," the foreign
posts in Romania had been abolished, and records the Prince himself attending the
opening of the international service at the post-and-telegraph palace
([[../../sources/istoria-postei-1916/text|Istoria Poștei 1916]] p.281). After
nearly forty years, the Russian and Austrian offices on Romanian soil were gone,
and the national post at last carried the country's mail to the world.

## Why this matters for the Bull's Head story

The 1858 Moldavian reform that produced the *Cap de bour* built a state post that
franked letters in advance and claimed a monopoly at home — yet that monopoly was
hollow at the border. For the whole first decade of the stamps' existence, the
most lucrative and prestigious traffic, the **international mail**, ran through
Austrian and Russian hands, sometimes carried free by the very post it competed
with. The Bull's Heads are therefore the emblem of a post fighting on two fronts:
inland, replacing the old [[menzil-relay-system|menzil relay system]] and private
concessions with a modern state service; and outward, struggling for a decade to
reclaim its own foreign mail. The stamps mark the beginning of that struggle; the
closure of the Austrian post on **31 March 1869** marks, in a sense, its end.

## Primary evidence — Wertheimer-Ghika 1944

The 1944 Wertheimer-Ghika study, drawing on the recovered Moldavian and
Wallachian postal archives, corroborates Minescu's picture and adds a more
document-grounded angle: less the political contest than the way the **Austrian
post penetrated the young Moldavian service from the inside**. Where the point is
genuinely new it is flagged; where it only confirms Minescu, that too is noted.

**A blunter restatement of the imbalance.** The book frames the situation as
Minescu does, but more sharply: in the era of the Ottoman Empire's wars with its
neighbours the Romanian lands had no communication with them „decât prin poștele
lor," except through the foreign posts, so that „poșta străină intra
nestingherită în Moldova și Muntenia; poșta țărilor românești se oprea la
graniță!" — the foreign post entered Moldavia and Wallachia unhindered, while the
post of the Romanian lands stopped at the border
([[../../sources/wertheimer-ghika-1944/text|Wertheimer-Ghika 1944]] photo 8). This
corroborates, in the book's own words, the existing account's point that the
national post was shut out of the international traffic (cf. Minescu p.228 above).

**A primary vignette: the Russian post used by Moldavia's own foreign minister.**
Wertheimer-Ghika reproduces a January 1859 letter from **Vasile Alecsandri** —
then Minister of Foreign Affairs (*ministru al trebilor streine*) of Moldavia — to
the chronicler **Gheorghe Sion**, dated „Iași, 20 Ghenar[ie] 1859," in which
Alecsandri asks Sion to „scrie-mi în particular prin poșta rusească," to write to
him privately through the Russian post
([[../../sources/wertheimer-ghika-1944/text|Wertheimer-Ghika 1944]] photos 8–9).
This is a **new** primary detail: on the very eve of the Union, Moldavia's own
foreign minister routed his private correspondence through the Russian office
rather than the national post — a concrete case of the dependence Minescu
describes only in the abstract.

**A dated Austrian travel document, August 1858.** Among „câteva date inedite"
(some unpublished data) the book prints Austrian post-administration records tied
to the arrival of an official named **Manowarda**, connected with the Austrian
consul-general at **Lemberg** (Lviv). One item, No. 234/Vb of **16 August 1858**,
runs from **Cernăuți** (Austrian Bukovina) to Iași and accounts for travel costs
in Austrian currency — e.g. „Synoutz pentru 3 poște 2 cai, à 7 Fl. 30 Kr. zilnic"
(7 Florins 30 Kreuzer daily), over the hand of a *Post-Verwalter*, dated Iași,
31 August 1858
([[../../sources/wertheimer-ghika-1944/text|Wertheimer-Ghika 1944]] photo 31).
These are **new, precisely dated** documentary traces of the Austrian post working
across the Bukovina–Moldavia frontier in the very weeks after the July 1858
Bull's Head issue.

**A border sales office at Darabani.** The book also notes an obscure office at
the market town of **Darabani**, on the Moldavia–Russia frontier near Herța in
Dorohoi county, which sold stamped paper and handled the receipt and despatch of
private letters
([[../../sources/wertheimer-ghika-1944/text|Wertheimer-Ghika 1944]] photo 31) — a
piece of the border postal geography absent from the existing account.

**The Austrians trained the Moldavian clerks.** The book's strongest new
contribution is documentary evidence of Austrian technical tutelage. The Moldavian
money-and-parcel form (the *Geld- und Frachten-Karte*), attested in a document of
**14 December 1858** and still in use at Vaslui and Galați in March 1860, is „o
fidelă copie" that renders „cuvânt cu cuvânt modelul austriac" — a faithful,
word-for-word copy and translation of the Austrian model, its values in Florins
and Kreuzer corrected by hand into Moldavian lei and parale, „introdus fiind de
funcționarii poștei austriace, angajați ca instructori ai funcționarilor
moldoveni" — introduced by Austrian postal officials engaged as instructors of the
Moldavian clerks
([[../../sources/wertheimer-ghika-1944/text|Wertheimer-Ghika 1944]] photo 60).
Wertheimer-Ghika suspects the same hands behind the Moldavian cancellations: the
*FRANCO* postmarks introduced in the autumn of 1859 betray „ortografia germană,"
German spelling, and „instructori austriaci nu par să fie străini nici ei de
această mică conspirațiune" — the Austrian instructors seem to be no strangers to
this little conspiracy either
([[../../sources/wertheimer-ghika-1944/text|Wertheimer-Ghika 1944]] photo 85). This
is **new**: it shows the Austrian post shaping the Moldavian service from within —
its forms, its currency conversions, even the spelling of its handstamps — a
dimension Minescu's political narrative does not reach. For the source itself see
[[../references/wertheimer-ghika-1944]].
