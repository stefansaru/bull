# Heraldry of Moldavia — the aurochs-head arms

The historic arms of the land of Moldavia are an **aurochs head seen from the
front** (Romanian *cap de bour*), a **star between its horns**, and — flanking
the head — a **flower / rose** and a **crescent moon**
([[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/002-symbol-of-moldavia|Narrative — Symbol of
Moldavia]]). This is the emblem that, five centuries after it first appears on
princely coins and seals, was engraved onto Moldavia's **first postage stamp**
in 1858 — the *Cap de bour* ([[../issues/issue-bull-1]]). This page traces how
that charge became, and remained, the arms of the country. The animal itself is
treated at [[aurochs]]; its broader symbolic afterlife at
[[aurochs-symbolism]]; and the recurring question of whether the beast is an
aurochs or a European bison at [[aurochs-vs-wisent]].

The founding **legend** of Dragoș and the aurochs hunt is told separately at
[[../events/dragos-founding-moldova]].

> [!legend]
> Tradition holds that the aurochs head entered the arms of Moldavia when Dragoș
> slew a great aurochs on the hunt and set up its severed head "for remembrance
> … on a stake on the riverbank" (Miron Costin, 1677)
> ([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.77). Tudor-Radu
> Tiron treats this as a *legendă cinegetică* (hunting legend), not documented
> fact, and cautions that it would be the only case in all medieval heraldry in
> which the victor identifies himself by the arms of the one he defeated
> ([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] pp.68–69). The
> account below follows what the seals and coins actually attest.

## Before Moldavia: the aurochs head in Hungarian and Maramureș heraldry

The bovid head was **not invented east of the Carpathians**. It was already a
relatively common charge in the heraldry of the medieval Kingdom of Hungary,
attested from the **13th century** and probably older
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.65). Its
prototype seems to be the aurochs head on the seal of **ban Buzád (1235)** and
of a *comes Tristanus (1255)*, from which descend the arms of several noble
lines of the old Buzáth-Hahót clan — the Csányi (a cross between the horns), the
Hahóthy (a star between the horns), the Bánffy of Alsólindva, and the Barlabási
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.65;
[[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/024-transylvanian-and-hungarian-seals|Narrative
— Transylvanian and Hungarian seals]]). Other families bore variants: the
counts **Balassa** (a bovid head with star and crescent) and the counts **Wass**
(a bovid head pierced by an arrow)
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.65). Tiron notes
the charge keeps two constant traits: (a) it is shown **frontally, usually
without a neck**, and (b) it can be **accompanied by auxiliary figures**,
generally pieces of the firmament (star, crescent) but also lilies or crosses
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.65). Its spread is
explained as a **hunting emblem** — the aurochs (or wisent) being the
"superlative" of royal and seigneurial hunts in 13th–14th-century Maramureș, so
that the trophy passed naturally into the hunter's personal arms
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.66).

Within **Maramureș**, the region the founders came from, the charge is
well-rooted. The **earliest attestation of a bovid head** there is on a document
of **1383** issued at Sighet, described by Ioan Mihály de Apșa as "the mark of
the town"; the same figure served as the usual emblem of both **Sighet** and
**Câmpulung-la-Tisa**
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] pp.63–64). A **1421**
seal bearing an aurochs head (with a star between the horns) is attributed to a
member of the **Drăgoșești** family
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.65). And the
chronicler **Miron Costin** already observed in 1677 that "Sighet, the capital
of Maramureș, to this day has as its seal the aurochs head, like this country
[Moldavia]" ([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.63).

> [!note]
> Whether the charge is an **aurochs (*bour*)** or a **wisent / European bison
> (*zimbru*)** is deliberately left open in the sources: Tiron writes the neutral
> *cap de bovideu* ("bovid head") "*indiferent de specia … zimbru sau bour*"
> ([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.65). The identity
> is `debated` — see [[aurochs-vs-wisent]].

## The founders' arms: the Drăgoșești and the Eski-Serai sword

The founding family carried **two** heraldic devices. The Drăgoșești used "their
own family arms" — an **arrow, a crescent and two stars** — a device Tiron dates
to the time of Dragoș or his immediate heirs, reconstructed from the many
14th–15th-century Moldavian boyar seals built on the arrow motif and from the
Galician *herb Sas* into which the family arms survive
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.69). The key
object linking these arms to the aurochs head is a two-handed sword now in the
**Eski-Serai** museum in Istanbul, whose pommel bears the **Drăgoșești arms on
one face and an aurochs head (with horns curved outward) on the other**
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.70). Tiron dates
the sword, by its shield type, to the **15th century** — but argues the pairing
shows the Drăgoșești could be credited with **both** devices, the arrow-arms and
the aurochs head, from the era of the founding
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] pp.70–71). Crucially,
the very same "formula" — shield on one side, aurochs head on the other — recurs
on the **coins of Moldavia struck from the reign of Petru I** onward
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.70).

Tiron resists the older idea of an **immemorial, prehistoric** aurochs emblem
east of the Carpathians: decorative bull protomes from the Eneolithic to the
Iron Age cannot be treated as credible sources for a *medieval heraldic* charge,
there being no intermediate evidence across the intervening millennia
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.68). The most
plausible explanation is **borrowing from Maramureș**, where the charge was
already established ([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]]
p.77).

## On the coinage: the groat of Petru Mușat (1375–1391)

Some of the **oldest surviving representations** of the arms of Moldavia are the
seals and coins of **Petru I (Petru Mușat, 1375–1391)**
([[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/027-the-groat|Narrative — The groat]]). The
silver **groat** (Romanian *gros*; the coinage circulated 1375–1665) carried on
its obverse an **aurochs head, a five-pointed star between the horns, a
five-petalled flower to the right, a crescent to the left**, within a pearled
ring — with the legend *+ SI PETRI … WOIWOI* (*SI[gillum] PETRI … WOIWO[d]I*, the
coin of Voivode Petru). The reverse showed a **split shield: three bars on the
left, seven fleurs-de-lys on the right**, with a lily above, legended *+ SI
MOLDAVIENSI* (*seal of Moldavia*)
([[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/027-the-groat|Narrative — The groat]]).

That "angevin" shield of **bars and lilies** is itself significant. Tiron reads
it not as an original Moldavian device but as a **reminiscence of the founding
period**, when Dragoș governed in the name of the Angevin king and "*sub
vexillo regio*"; the same royal bars-and-lilies survive, by sheer inertia, in
the arms of Transylvanian-Saxon towns such as Bistrița and in many other
frontier communities ([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron
2020]] pp.72–73). He is **skeptical of the theory** that Petru I received these
arms as a formal Angevin *concession of arms* tied to the right to mint,
following Gorovei in judging the supporting evidence thin
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] pp.72–73). On the
monochrome coins, he stresses, no colour distinction from the Hungarian royal
arms could have been conveyed at all
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.73).

## The seal of Roman I (1392–1393)

The next fixed point is the **seal of Roman I Mușat (ruled 1392–1394)**. A
charter of donation to Ionaș the Brave, issued **30 March 1392** in Roman I's
citadel, is the first documentary attestation of the city of Roman
([[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/028-seal-of-roman-i|Narrative — Seal of Roman
I]]). On this seal the **crescent stands to the right and the flower to the
left**, and the **horn-tips are curved outward** — matching the archaic form
seen on the Eski-Serai sword and the early coins, though the moon sits on the
*opposite* side from where it appears on the coin, a small index of how the
device shifted between minting and sealing practice
([[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/028-seal-of-roman-i|Narrative — Seal of Roman
I]]).

## The rose / flower and the crescent: the attributes and their debates

Beside the star between the horns, the arms carry a **flower (later a rose)** and
a **crescent**. Tiron treats the rose as part of a symbolic "series" that
accompanies the aurochs head **from the earliest coins and seals**, not a late
addition ([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.75). He
surveys the competing origins for it:

- **A Mușatin family device.** Jean N. Mănescu and Dan Cernovodeanu supposed the
  rose was the old emblem of the Mușatin house (Bogdan's line)
  ([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.75).
- **The papal Golden Rose.** Ștefan S. Gorovei asked whether the charge derived
  from the jewelled golden rose that Pope **Sixtus IV** may have sent **Stephen
  the Great around 1475** as *Athleta Christi* — historically plausible, Tiron
  grants, but the rose is already part of the older heraldic series
  ([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.75).
- **The rose of the Pok family.** Tiron's own hypothesis: the rose was the arms
  of the **Pok** family, political allies of Bogdan against the Drăgoșești in
  Maramureș; carried east, it lost its factional meaning and joined the other
  charges. He notes that **adding a rose to the arms of Câmpulung-la-Tisa yields
  exactly the heraldic formula of the arms of Moldavia, valid to this day**
  ([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] pp.75–76). A
  recently found seal-matrix reading *† SGI BOGANI WEWOD* bearing a heraldic
  rose has been attributed to Bogdan I
  ([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.75).

The **crescent** belongs to the same "pieces of the firmament" that
conventionally accompany the bovid head across Hungarian heraldry
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.65); on the
Moldavian arms it and the star are shown to **predate Stephen the Great** and to
have continued in use "throughout the period of Ottoman influence without …
being a direct reference to it"
([[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/030-putna-monastery|Narrative — Putna
Monastery]]).

## Dragoș or Bogdan? Continuity, not rupture

A long historiographic dispute asks whether the aurochs head was assumed by
**Dragoș** (the first founder) or brought from Maramureș by **Bogdan** (the
second) ([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] pp.67, 74).
Tiron favours **continuity**: because internal succession begins with Dragoș,
and because elements like the "angevin" shield can only have come from a ruler in
real subordination to the Hungarian crown, he traces the princely heraldry back
to **Dragoș**, with the Bogdănești line merely conserving what was well
established ([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.75).
Even so, he presumes Bogdan too used the aurochs head — the symbol being already
familiar throughout the region the founders came from
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] pp.74–75).

## Stephen the Great and Putna: consolidation

Under **Stephen the Great (1457–1504)** the arms are fully consolidated on seals,
coins and monastery stonework
([[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/029-stephen-the-great|Narrative — Stephen the
Great]]). On Stephen's seal the **moon and flower sit on the same side as on
Roman I's seal**, but the **horns are no longer curved outward** — they take a
form resembling those of a **European bison (wisent)**
([[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/029-stephen-the-great|Narrative — Stephen the
Great]]). The book is explicit that this stylistic drift **does not by itself
mean** the animal has changed from aurochs to bison; heraldry shifts with
artistic fashion, changing rules, or a ruler's preference — an instance of the
same `debated` identity question at [[aurochs-vs-wisent]]
([[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/029-stephen-the-great|Narrative — Stephen the
Great]]).

At **Putna Monastery** — Stephen's foundation and burial place — the arms carry a
**cross on the aurochs's forehead**, read in Stephen's context as a mark of
**Christianity**, fitting his reputation as a defender of the faith against the
Ottomans; but, again, the **star and crescent between the horns predate Stephen**
and are not a claim over the Ottoman Empire
([[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/030-putna-monastery|Narrative — Putna
Monastery]]).

## Continuity into the modern era and the 1858 stamp

The formula fixed on the earliest coins and seals — **aurochs head, star between
the horns, rose and crescent** — is the same one Tiron calls the heraldic formula
of the arms of Moldavia "**valid to this day**"
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] p.76). That unbroken
continuity is precisely why, when the principality issued its **first postage
stamps in 1858**, the design chosen was the *Cap de bour* — the aurochs head with
its attributes, the arms of the land itself
([[../issues/issue-bull-1]];
[[../../narrative/02-the-aurochs/002-symbol-of-moldavia|Narrative — Symbol of
Moldavia]]). The stamp is thus not a new invention but the latest voice of a
device that runs from the Maramureș seals of the 1380s, through the groat of
Petru Mușat and the seal of Roman I, to Stephen the Great and beyond
([[../../sources/tiron-2020-stema-moldovei/text|Tiron 2020]] pp.63–77).

## On the post's own seals, 1822–1858

There is a nearer link between the medieval arms and the 1858 stamp than the coins
and monastery stonework: the **Moldavian post was already striking the bull's head
on its own seals** for decades before the *Cap de bour*. Kiriac Dragomir's
*Ștampilografie Poștală* catalogues the postal seals from **1822** onward and states
the principle plainly — *"the principal element of the heraldic seals of Moldavia is
the cap de bour, and of Wallachia the eagle"* — adding that the bull's head *"appears
for the first time **in philately** on the cap de bour stamps"* of 22 July and 1
November 1858 ([[../../sources/dragomir-stampilografie-1990/text|Ștampilografie]]
photo 10). The qualifier *in philately* is the point: on the **seals** the charge was
in continuous postal use well before.

- The **1830 seal of the Casa Menzilului Moldovei** ([[../handstamps/SM30|SM30]])
  already shows the bull's head with a **star between the horns** and flanking
  branches — the arms of the land on the relay-post's own die
  ([[../../sources/dragomir-stampilografie-1990/text|Ștampilografie]] Plate 1).
- The **1858 seal of the Directorate of Posts and Diligences**
  ([[../handstamps/SM58|SM58]]) carries the full achievement — a shield with the
  *cap de bour*, a closed crown, two flanking dolphins and a postal horn dated 1858
  — struck in the very year the stamp was issued
  ([[../../sources/dragomir-stampilografie-1990/text|Ștampilografie]] Fig. 28).
- After the 1859 Union, the Iași bureau seal (`SM59`) sets the **Moldavian bull's
  head beside the Wallachian eagle** under the legend "PRINCIPATELE UNITE" — the same
  pairing that later appears on the *Principatele Unite* stamps
  ([[../../sources/dragomir-stampilografie-1990/text|Ștampilografie]] Fig. 32).

So the 1858 stamp did not reach for a museum emblem; it reproduced the charge the
Moldavian post handled every day. The pre-stamp seals are catalogued at
[[../concepts/pre-stamp-postal-markings]] and [[../handstamps/README|the device index]].
