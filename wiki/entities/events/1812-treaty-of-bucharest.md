# 1812 — Treaty of Bucharest & the loss of Bessarabia

> [!info] Why this page exists
> This is a page about the **effects of a historical decision**. On one May day in
> 1812, in the courtyard rooms of a Bucharest inn, two empires drew a line down the
> middle of a country neither of them was. The line — the **Prut river** — is still
> an international border today, and the land east of it is now the **Republic of
> Moldova**. Almost nothing else on this wiki reaches so directly from a single
> signature into the present. See [[../concepts/geopolitics-1800s]] for the wider
> board on which this move was played, and [[../concepts/why-bull-heads-matter]]
> for why a severed Moldavian province still matters to the story of the stamp.

## The decision, in one sentence

Following the **Russo-Turkish War of 1806–1812**, the peace treaty of 1812 was
signed at **Manuc's Inn (Hanul lui Manuc)** in Bucharest, and as a result the
territory of **Bessarabia (Basarabia)**, between the **Prut** and the **Nistru
(Dniester)** rivers, was carved out of Moldavia and ceded to Russia
([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/006-1812-peace-treaty-bucharest]]). The
Romanian historical literature states it bluntly: *"Imperiul Rus a rupt în 1812
din trupul Moldovei partea dintre Prut și Nistru (Basarabia)"* — the Russian
Empire **tore from the body of Moldavia** the part between the Prut and the
Dniester (Carol I & Patru Regi, p.18–19).

## The war that ended here

The war was one link in the long chain of Russo-Turkish conflicts, as the Russian
Empire pushed for influence in the Balkans and control of the Black Sea littoral
([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/005-1806-russo-turkish-war-moldavia-wallachia]]).
Its trigger was Russia's objection to the Ottoman deposition of the pro-Russian
Phanariot princes — Constantine Ypsilantis in Wallachia and Alexander Moruzi in
Moldavia. Tsar **Alexander I** used the dethronements as a pretext, and Russian
forces occupied both principalities in late 1806
([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/005-1806-russo-turkish-war-moldavia-wallachia]]).

The war dragged on for six years, with **Moldavia and Wallachia as its theatre** —
requisitions, destruction, and occupation falling on the local population. A
decisive Russian victory came at the **Battle of Slobozia (1811)**
([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/005-1806-russo-turkish-war-moldavia-wallachia]]).
By 1812 both powers wanted out for the same larger reason: **Napoleon**. Russia
needed its army free to meet the coming French invasion, and the Ottomans wanted no
part of the Napoleonic wars [1]. Alexander I ratified the treaty just **thirteen
days before Napoleon crossed into Russia** [1].

## The terms

The treaty was signed on **16/28 May 1812** and **ratified on 5 July 1812** [1]. The
Russian side was led by the commander **Mikhail Kutuzov**; the Ottoman delegation
was headed by **Mehmed Said Galib Efendi** [1]. The core provisions:

- **Cession of Bessarabia.** The eastern half of the Principality of Moldavia,
  between the **Prut** and the **Dniester**, an area of about **45,630 km²**, passed
  from Ottoman suzerainty to the Russian Empire [1][2]. The new boundary ran along
  the Prut from where it enters Moldavia down to its junction with the **Danube**,
  advancing the Russian frontier to the northern mouth of the Danube at **Kilia** [1][5].
- **What Russia gave back.** Russia was required to **return Wallachia and the rest
  of Moldavia** (west of the Prut), which its armies had occupied [1][2]. It kept
  only the land east of the river.
- **Scale of the transfer.** Roughly **half a million people**, five fortresses and
  hundreds of villages changed sovereignty in a single stroke [1].
- **Other clauses.** Russia obtained **trading rights on the Danube**, and the
  rebelling **Serbs** were promised amnesty and a measure of autonomy [1][2].

The Russians named their acquisition **"Bessarabia,"** a label borrowed and
stretched from a smaller historic district; it is the name the whole territory has
carried ever since [5].

## Where it was signed: Hanul lui Manuc

The treaty was concluded at **Manuc's Inn (Hanul lui Manuc)**, built **1806–1808**
by the wealthy Armenian merchant, diplomat and boyar **Manuc Bei** (Emanuel
Mârzaian / Manuc Mârzayan, 1769–1817) [3][4]. It was conceived as a **caravanserai**
— a self-contained world of ground-floor shops, upper-floor rooms for merchants and
their goods, a great arcaded courtyard for carriages and horses, and heavy gates
barred at night. By the mid-19th century it held more than a hundred rooms, dozens
of wholesalers and retailers, reception halls and a pub — Bucharest's premier
commercial complex [3]. In the spring of 1812 the decisive negotiations between the
Russian and Ottoman delegations were held **in its halls**, and the treaty was
signed there [3].

> [!legend] Tradition vs. documented fact (convention C-17)
> Manuc Bei's own end is wrapped in legend rather than record. He died suddenly on
> his estate at **Hâncești** in **1817**, aged 48, and was buried by the Armenian
> church in **Chișinău** [4]. Popular tales — including those recounted by the inn
> itself today — say he was **poisoned** during his time as a refugee in Russia, or
> that he **fell from a horse with a "little help" from the Russians**, one version
> adding that his saddle straps had been cut [4][6]. The inn's own account concedes
> the archives hold *no clear details* and that the "real truth" may never be known
> [6]. These are **legends about a death**, not supernatural lore; no prophecy,
> curse or ghost is documented for the building. What *is* documented is the
> treaty, and that is what gives the inn its lasting fame [6].

## The effect: a land severed, and its long shadow

This is the point of the page. A boundary agreed by two empires — in a country that
was represented at the table by neither — became a **fault line that has not
closed in more than two centuries.**

- **1812 → immediately.** The cession detached a Romanian-speaking province from the
  rest of Moldavia. Emigration across the Prut began at once; roughly **30,000
  peasants** are said to have crossed into western Moldavia in the first wave, and
  under Russian rule the Romanian language was progressively pushed out of
  administration and church in favour of **russification** [7].
- **1856.** After the Crimean War, the **Treaty of Paris** clawed back a strip of
  southern Bessarabia — the counties of **Cahul, Bolgrad and Ismail** — to Moldavia
  ([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/037-1856-treaty-paris]],
  [[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/038-1856-new-counties-in-moldavia-cahul-bolgrad-ismail]]),
  a partial and temporary reversal of 1812.
- **1918.** As the Russian Empire collapsed, the assembly (**Sfatul Țării**) of the
  Moldavian Democratic Republic voted **union with Romania** — a direct attempt to
  undo 1812 [7].
- **1940.** Under the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact's secret protocol, the USSR forced
  Romania to **cede Bessarabia** again; it became, in altered borders, the Moldavian
  SSR [5].
- **1991 → today.** On the collapse of the Soviet Union, the core of Bessarabia
  emerged as the independent **Republic of Moldova**, while its northern and
  southern fringes remained with **Ukraine** [5]. The **Prut is still the border**,
  and the "Bessarabian question" — the relationship between Romania and Moldova — is
  a live political theme to this day [5][7].

Every one of those later dates is, in a real sense, a **reply to the signature of
1812**. That is the theme this wiki wants to make explicit: decisions taken in a
single room outlast the men who take them, sometimes by centuries.

## The building today

The inn **survived** every one of those upheavals. Restored repeatedly — after
mid-19th-century remodellings and through campaigns dated to **1966–1970,
1991–1992 and 2007** — its essential structure stayed intact [3]. It briefly served
as Bucharest's **town hall in 1842** and hosted an early Romanian **operetta** around
1880 [3]. Today, in the heart of Bucharest's **Old Town (Lipscani)**, **Hanul lui
Manuc** is a category-A historic monument and Bucharest's oldest functioning inn
building: a restaurant of traditional Romanian cuisine, bars and a coffee house,
shops and guest lodging around the same arcaded courtyard [3]. The book's own photos
run the contrast directly — an **1841 lithograph** of the inn beside a **2012
photograph** and views of the working restaurant of **today**
([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/006-1812-peace-treaty-bucharest]]).

So the place where a Romanian province was signed away still stands, restored and
busy — a landmark you can eat dinner in, sitting a few metres from where the border
of a nation was moved. For the building itself, see [[../places/hanul-lui-manuc]].
