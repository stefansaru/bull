# 📅 The Crimean War & the Treaty of Paris (1853-1856)

> [!summary]
> The Crimean War turned Moldavia and Wallachia into occupied borderland twice
> over — first by Russia, then by Austria — but its peace, the **Treaty of Paris
> of 1856**, ended the long Russian protectorate, placed the Principalities under
> the **collective guarantee of the Great Powers**, and created the consultative
> **ad-hoc Divans** that let Romanians vote for Union. It is the diplomatic hinge
> between the [[1812-treaty-of-bucharest]] and the
> [[union-of-principalities-1859]] — and thus the political backdrop against which
> Moldavia issued its 1858 **Bull's Head** stamps. See
> [[../concepts/geopolitics-1800s]] and [[../concepts/postal-history-romania]].

## The outbreak (1853)

By the mid-19th century the declining Ottoman Empire — the "sick man of Europe" —
had made the **Eastern Question** the central quarrel of the Great Powers. A
dispute over the protection of Christian minorities in Ottoman lands, with Russia
claiming to speak for the Orthodox and France for the Roman Catholics, escalated
in 1852-1853 into open confrontation
([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/026-1853-crimean-war-begins-wallachia-moldavia]]).
When the Sultan, encouraged by Britain and France, refused Russia's demands, in
**mid-1853 Russian troops moved into the Danubian Principalities** of Moldavia and
Wallachia to force Ottoman compliance; on **4 October 1853 the Ottomans declared
war**, beginning what became the Crimean War
([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/026-1853-crimean-war-begins-wallachia-moldavia]]).

The war reached the Principalities almost immediately: the **Battle of Oltenița**
(November 1853) was its first major engagement, and the Russian navy's destruction
of the Ottoman fleet at **Sinop** secured the Black Sea but provoked Britain and
France ([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/027-1853-exile-of-ghica-and-ştirbey-wallachia-moldavia-vienna]]).

## The Russian occupation (1853-1854)

With the Russian entry the two ruling princes — **Barbu Știrbey** of Wallachia and
**Grigore Ghica** of Moldavia — were forced into **exile in Vienna** (October
1853). Tsar Nicholas I placed the Principalities under a special governor, General
Adjutant **Baron Budberg**, assisted by two vice-presidents, Kalcinski in
Bucharest and Uruzov in Iași
([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/027-1853-exile-of-ghica-and-ştirbey-wallachia-moldavia-vienna]]).
The Russian entry sharpened the rivalry of the Powers over the Principalities and
pushed the "Romanian question" into the front rank of European diplomacy
([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea Principatelor, vol. 2]] p.15).

## The Austrian occupation and the Russian withdrawal (1854)

In **March 1854 Britain and France declared war on Russia**, siding with the
Ottomans to check Russian expansion
([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/028-1854-austrian-occupation-wallachia-moldavia]]).
The decisive pressure on the Principalities, however, came from **Austria**, which
feared that a strengthened Russian presence in the Balkans would encourage Slavic
revolts within its own empire and therefore demanded a Russian withdrawal. Under
the **Boiagi-Kioi agreement (2/14 June 1854)** between Austria and the Porte,
**Austria temporarily occupied the Danubian Principalities** to guarantee that
withdrawal ([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/028-1854-austrian-occupation-wallachia-moldavia]]).

Facing the threat of Austria entering the war outright, **Tsar Nicholas I ordered
his troops to retreat**; the fighting shifted to Crimea itself, and the
Principalities were left **at peace but under Austrian military control**
([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/029-1854-russian-withdrawal-wallachia-moldavia]]).
This second occupation would outlast the war. Austria opposed any unification —
keeping the two countries weak, divided and nominally Ottoman so as to prevent a
united Romanian state from inspiring the Romanians of Habsburg Transylvania — and
its agents worked openly for the **separatist** cause even as the fighting ended
([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/040-1856-still-under-austrian-occupation-moldavia-wallachia]];
[[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea Principatelor, vol. 2]] p.19).
Vienna's hopes ran further still: through 1855-1856 Austrian diplomacy floated
schemes to **annex the two countries outright** or to swap them for Lombardy under
an Austrian archduke — plans that the course of events would frustrate
([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea Principatelor, vol. 2]] p.15).

## The Treaty of Paris (18/30 March 1856)

The war ended in defeat for Russia at the hands of France, Britain, the Ottoman
Empire and Sardinia, and was concluded by the **Treaty of Paris signed on 30 March
1856**. Its terms sharply curtailed Russia's position in Europe, in particular its
access to the **Black Sea** and the **Danube**
([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/037-1856-treaty-paris]]). For the
Principalities the settlement was transformative on four connected points:

- **End of the Russian protectorate.** The exclusive Russian tutelage established
  after earlier Russo-Turkish wars — the framework that had run through the
  [[1812-treaty-of-bucharest]] and the Organic Regulations — was replaced by a
  new international regime
  ([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/037-1856-treaty-paris]];
  [[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea Principatelor, vol. 2]] p.15).
- **Collective guarantee of the Great Powers.** The Principalities were placed
  **under the guarantee of the Great Powers** — Britain, France, Austria, Russia,
  the Ottoman Empire, Prussia and Sardinia — the seven signatory states
  collectively replacing the single Russian guarantor
  ([[../../sources/carol-i-patru-regi/text|Carol I (Patru regi)]] p.22).
- **Return of southern Bessarabia to Moldavia.** Russia was compelled to cede the
  three southern Bessarabian districts — **Cahul, Bolgrad and Ismail** — to the
  Principality of **Moldavia**, deliberately pushing Russia back from the mouths of
  the Danube and depriving it of direct access to the river
  ([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/038-1856-new-counties-in-moldavia-cahul-bolgrad-ismail]]).
  This partly reversed the losses of 1812 (see [[1812-treaty-of-bucharest]]); the
  three districts would be lost again in 1878.
- **The ad-hoc Divans — the mechanism for Union.** Article 24 of the Treaty
  required the Porte to convene at once, in each Principality, an **ad-hoc Divan**
  that would form "the most exact representation of the interests of all classes of
  society" and would be called to **express the wishes of the populations** on the
  definitive organisation of the Principalities. A special commission of the
  signatory states would examine the two assemblies' opinions and report back to
  **Paris**, where the Great Powers would decide
  ([[../../sources/carol-i-patru-regi/text|Carol I (Patru regi)]] p.22).

## Why it set the stage for Union — and for the Bull's Head

The ad-hoc Divans, elected in 1857, gave the unionist movement a legal platform:
the assemblies at **Iași and Bucharest voted on 7/19 and 9/21 October 1857 for the
union of the two countries into a single state**
([[../../sources/carol-i-patru-regi/text|Carol I (Patru regi)]] p.22). Austria,
still occupying the Principalities, resisted union both diplomatically and by
backing the separatists ([[../../sources/unirea-principatelor-1959-vol2/text|Unirea Principatelor, vol. 2]] p.19),
but its occupation ended in **1857** under Franco-British pressure, clearing the
ground for the pro-unionist current
([[../../narrative/03-xixth-century/043-1857-austrian-withdrawal-wallachia-moldavia]]).
The road ran from here through the 1858 Convention of Paris to the double election
of Alexandru Ioan Cuza in 1859 — the [[union-of-principalities-1859]].

It was precisely in this window — a Moldavia freed from the Russian protectorate,
newly guaranteed by Europe, and asserting itself on the eve of Union — that the
principality's postal service issued its own stamps: the **1858 Bull's Head**.
For that postal context see [[../concepts/postal-history-romania]]; for the wider
diplomatic frame see [[../concepts/geopolitics-1800s]].
