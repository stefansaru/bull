# 🏛️ Hanul lui Manuc (Manuc's Inn), Bucharest

> [!info] Why this page exists
> Most of the rooms where this wiki's history happened are gone — demolished,
> built over, or unmarked (see [[old-post-buildings]]). **Hanul lui Manuc** is the
> great exception: the caravanserai where the [[../events/1812-treaty-of-bucharest|1812
> Treaty of Bucharest]] was negotiated still stands in the middle of the city,
> restored and busy, a place a reader can walk into and eat dinner in. It is the
> clearest "historical building today" link on the whole board. The treaty itself
> — the ceded province, the border that never closed — is told on its own page;
> this page is about the **building**.

## Who Manuc Bei was

The inn is named for its builder, **Manuc Bei** — the common Romanian rendering of
the Armenian name **Emanuel Mârzaian** (Manuc Mârzayan), born in **1769** in
**Rusçuk** (modern Ruse, Bulgaria), then part of the Ottoman Empire [2]. He rose
as a grain merchant and accumulated a fortune so large he was **rumoured to be the
wealthiest man in the Balkans** [2]. Wealth bought rank and access: in **1803** the
Prince of Wallachia, Constantine Ypsilanti, granted him the boyar title of
**paharnic**, and on **24 October 1808**, by firman of **Sultan Mahmud II**, Manuc
was recognised as **Grand Dragoman**, Deputy Minister of Foreign Affairs and Grand
Treasurer to the Sublime Porte [2].

That double life — Armenian merchant and Ottoman official — placed him inside the
diplomacy of the [[../concepts/geopolitics-1800s|empires manoeuvring over the
Principalities]]. During the Russo-Turkish War of 1806–1812 he acted as a
**mediator (1809)** and took part in the negotiations for the
[[../events/1812-treaty-of-bucharest|1812 peace]] [2]. When his Ottoman protector
Alemdar Mustafa Pasha fell from power in late 1808, Manuc had to **flee Istanbul to
avoid execution**, and he ended his days on estates he acquired in Bessarabia, near
**Hîncești and Reni** [2].

> [!legend] Tradition vs. documented fact (convention C-17)
> **Manuc Bei died in an accident in 1817**, aged 48, and was buried in the
> **Armenian church in Chișinău** — that much is documented [2]. Around that bare
> fact popular tradition has spun colour: the inn's own telling says he may have
> been **poisoned** during his Russian exile, or **fell from a horse with a
> "little help" from the Russians** — one version adding that his **saddle straps
> had been cut** — while conceding the archives hold *no clear details* and the
> "real truth" may never be known [6]. These are **legends about a death**, not
> ghosts or curses; the building carries no supernatural lore. What is beyond
> dispute is the treaty signed under its roof, and that is what made it famous [6].

## When and why the han was built

Manuc built his inn in Bucharest **between 1806 and 1808** — most sources give
**1808** as the completion — as a **khan (caravanserai)**: a fortified commercial
inn of the Ottoman-Balkan world, a self-contained enclave for travelling merchants
and their goods [1][5]. Bucharest sat on the trade routes between the Ottoman south
and Central Europe, and a wealthy merchant-diplomat building the city's grandest
han was a natural investment. It went up on the slope below the old princely court,
in what is today the **Lipscani** quarter — the historic trading heart of the city.

## What it was: a fortified inn and a small city

The han was conceived as a **four-sided block enclosing a great inner courtyard**,
occupying roughly **3,000 m²**, blending the local **Brâncovenesc** style with the
Oriental caravanserai [1][5]. Its plan reads like a small self-sufficient town:

- A **basement of vaulted cellars** — deep and large, some counts giving fifteen —
  for storing wine and merchandise [5].
- A **ground floor of shops and warehouses**, kitchens and servants' quarters,
  opening to the street and the yard [1][5].
- **Upper floors of rooms** for merchants and lodgers. By the middle of the 19th
  century the complex tallied **15 wholesalers, 23 retail stores, 107 rooms for
  offices or living, two reception halls and a pub** — Bucharest's single most
  important commercial building [1].
- The courtyard was ringed by **double open galleries on two levels, carried on
  carved wooden pillars** — an arcade drawn straight from old Wallachian domestic
  architecture, and now an extremely rare survival of that vernacular in the city
  [5]. Above the entrance gate sits a **glazed veranda**, reckoned the finest such
  original structure in Bucharest [5].

Heavy gates, ranks of shops facing outward and lodgings turned inward toward a
guarded court — this was a **fortified inn**, sheltering caravans and their
carriages behind barred doors at night. One further quirk marks its age: it is the
**only shingle-roofed building in central Bucharest**, a roofing so flammable that
the City Hall **banned it after the Great Fire of 1847** — yet the han kept its
original roof, having come through the fire that levelled much of the city [5].

## Its role in 1812

In the spring of 1812, the han's halls hosted the **preliminary negotiations** that
ended the 1806–1812 Russo-Turkish war, and the **Treaty of Bucharest** was
concluded there [1] — the peace that tore **Bessarabia** from Moldavia and handed
it to the Russian Empire. Manuc himself, mediator and Ottoman official, was one of
the figures moving around those talks [2]. The full story of the treaty, its terms
and its two-century shadow — a border on the **Prut** that is still an
international line today — is told on its own page:
[[../events/1812-treaty-of-bucharest]].

## Survival through disasters and restorations

The building's long life is a chronicle of catastrophe survived. It came through
the **Great Fire of 1847** with its shingle roof intact [5], and stood through the
earthquakes that flattened neighbours across two centuries. It was **repeatedly
restored** — after remodellings in **1848** and **1863**, then in **1966–1970**,
**1991–1992**, and most thoroughly in **2007** [1][4]. Along the way it wore other
uses: it briefly **housed Bucharest's town hall in 1842**, its courtyard staged the
**first Romanian operetta** around **1880**, and its "Sala Dacia" hall hosted
**pro-unification meetings during 1914–1916** in the drive toward Greater Romania
[1].

Politics nearly did what fire and earthquake could not. The inn was **nationalised
on 19 February 1949**; decades later, after a long restitution process, ownership
was **restored to the Cantacuzino family** — Prince Șerban-Constantin Cantacuzino —
in **February 2007** [1]. The refurbishment that followed **replaced the yard
paving and the wooden beams to bring back the original Brâncovenesc look**, and the
cellar, restaurant, hotel and summer garden were restored to convey the inn's
medieval charm [4].

## What stands there today

Today **Hanul lui Manuc** is a restored, category-A historic monument in the heart
of the **Old Town (Lipscani)** and, by common account, the **only one of the
surviving 19th-century Bucharest inns still working as a hotel** [1]. Around the
same great arcaded courtyard it runs a **restaurant of traditional Romanian
cuisine**, several **bars**, a **coffee house**, street-facing **shops**, and
guest **lodging**; the courtyard itself still hosts performances and events, and
the han bills itself as **"the last caravanserai of South-Eastern Europe"** [1][3][5].

That is the connection this wiki wants to make plain. Where the old post buildings
of the [[../concepts/bull-heads-today|Bull's Head story]] are mostly vanished or
unmarked (see [[old-post-buildings]]), Manuc's Inn is the rare survivor you can
step fully into — a working restaurant a few metres from the room where the border
of a nation was moved.
