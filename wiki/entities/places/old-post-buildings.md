# 📯 Historic post buildings — then and today

> [!note]
> This page keeps two things apart on purpose. What the buildings **were** in the
> 1850s–1900s postal story is drawn from the book and the documentary blog
> [[../../sources/bucurestiul-uitat-posta-veche/text|Poșta veche]]. What **stands
> there today** — museum, working post office, restored inn, or empty ground — is
> from present-day web sources, cited `[n]`. Where a building is gone, this page
> says so.

The 1858 [[../concepts/postal-history-romania|Bull's Head]] reform did not happen
in the abstract: it happened in rooms, courtyards and stables that a traveller in
Bucharest or Iași could walk into. Some of those places survive as landmarks; one
now guards the original stamp dies; others have vanished under later construction.

## Bucharest — "Poșta Veche" behind the Athenaeum (gone)

The oldest post house of Bucharest — called at the time not "old" but the
**Poșta Românească** or **Poșta de Trăsuri** ("post of carriages") — stood on the
street *behind the Athenaeum*, at the start of Calea Dorobanți, on a lane then
named **Ulița Poșta Veche**: today's **Str. Nicolae Golescu**, at the corner with
Str. George Enescu ([[../../sources/bucurestiul-uitat-posta-veche/text|Poșta veche]] p.3, p.5).
The property was large — reaching east toward Str. Pitar Moși and south past
today's Str. Pictor Verona — and more than half of it was taken up by the
**Grădina Poștei**, the "post garden"
([[../../sources/bucurestiul-uitat-posta-veche/text|Poșta veche]] p.3). It got its
retrospective nickname *Poșta Veche* only once the central post moved out; the
street later carried the name too
([[../../sources/bucurestiul-uitat-posta-veche/text|Poșta veche]] p.2).

**Today:** nothing of the post survives here. The block sits in the busy George
Enescu / Nicolae Golescu district behind the Romanian Athenaeum, and the blog's
whole point is to correct a persistent local myth — the surviving old houses at
Str. Nicolae Golescu 4/6 (part of the former *Schitul Călugărițelor catolice*)
were **not** the Poșta Veche, which lay further north
([[../../sources/bucurestiul-uitat-posta-veche/text|Poșta veche]] p.5). The site
is a place to imagine, not to visit.

## Bucharest — the Str. Doamnei post (demolished)

In **1867** the central post moved to **Str. Doamnei no. 11**, at the corner with
Str. Vestei (the future Str. Ion Ghica), into one of the houses of the
**Bărcănescu family**; from here the Directorate-General of Posts eventually moved
on to the new Post Palace
([[../../sources/bucurestiul-uitat-posta-veche/text|Poșta veche]] p.3).

**Today:** demolished. After the building came down, the empty lot became the
**Basilescu summer garden**, one of Bucharest's open-air cinemas — the businessman
Mitică Georgescu opening the *Parcul Bazilescu*, with a free cinematograph, on the
cleared ground in 1905 — before the plot was built over by the new
**Palatul Bursei** (Stock Exchange)
([[../../sources/bucurestiul-uitat-posta-veche/text|Poșta veche]] p.3).

## Bucharest — the Palatul Poștelor → National Museum of Romanian History

When the service outgrew Str. Doamnei, the state built a purpose-designed
**Palatul Poștelor** (Post Palace). After considering a riverside plot near the
State Printing House, the site was fixed on **Calea Victoriei**, between the Carol
and Stavropoleos streets
([[../../sources/bucurestiul-uitat-posta-veche/text|Poșta veche]] p.4). Funding
shortages dragged the work out; director **Mihail Ghica** secured an extraordinary
credit in May 1899 for electric lighting and lifts, and between October 1899 and
April 1900 the technical, accounting and then the remaining central divisions —
postal, telegraph and telephone — moved in
([[../../sources/bucurestiul-uitat-posta-veche/text|Poșta veche]] p.4).

**Today:** the palace still stands at **Calea Victoriei 12**, but it is no longer a
post office. Designed by architect **Alexandru Săvulescu** in a neoclassical style
inspired by the Geneva post office and built 1894–1900, it served as Bucharest's
central post office **until 1970**; after conversion, the **National Museum of
Romanian History (MNIR)** opened inside in 1972 [1][2]. This makes it the single
most important building for the Bull's Head story: the museum's **Philatelic
Collection** holds *all the original dies* of the 1858 Bull's Head issues, plus an
unissued die, together with lithographic stones and postal stationery documenting
the manufacturing stages [3]. Curator Cristina Antonie confirms the museum has "all
the original dies," though there is currently **no permanent philatelic exhibition**
— space limits and an ongoing inventory keep the collection largely in store, with
a printed catalogue of the dies available instead [3]. The building itself has been
a near-permanent construction site: a large-scale consolidation, restoration and
modernization was contracted from 2024 at an estimated cost of over €100 million,
with works expected to run several years and no firm reopening date announced [7].
For the wider fate of the stamps and dies, see [[../concepts/bull-heads-today]].

## Iași — the 1857–58 central office, and the surviving post landmark

The Bull's Head was a **Moldavian** issue, and its administrative home was Iași.
In April 1857 **Kostache Bosie** signed a contract with the entrepreneur
**Theodor Ghica**, renting 14 rooms in several of Ghica's houses *opposite the
Orphanotropy Institute (Institutul de Orfanotropie)*; this became the seat of the
**Central Postal Office** after the 1858 reform
([[../04-1858/018-1858-dpd-and-central-postal-office-iaşi-jassy|1858 D.P.D. & Central Postal Office, Iași]];
[[../03-xixth-century/049-1857-new-postal-headquarters-iaşi-jassy|1857 New Postal Headquarters, Iași]]).
From **1 May 1858** both the Postal Directorate (D.P.D.) and the Central Postal
Office operated from these rented rooms — the very offices where, months later, the
chief cashier **Neculai Costin** was found to have run a stamp deficit and, on the
night of 3–4 February 1859, hanged himself in a carriage in the chancery courtyard
([[../05-1859/005-1859-1-investigation-and-tragedy-at-the-post-office-iaşi-jas|Investigation and Tragedy at the Post Office, Iași]]).

**Today:** the rented Ghica rooms of 1857–58 are not marked or identifiable as a
monument. The building that Ieșeni now associate with the post is a different and
later one — the **Casa Balș-Sturdza** at **Str. Cuza Vodă no. 3**, a listed
historic monument built in **1842** that in turn housed the Bank of Moldavia, later
the Iași branch of the National Bank, and after World War II the **Poșta Română**
[6]. Of "exceptional architecture and great historical value," it has needed
repairs for years and was put up for lease by the post company [6] — a working but
fragile survivor, distinct from the vanished 1858 offices.

## Bucharest — Hanul lui Manuc (restored, and open)

Not a post building, but the most vivid surviving structure of the whole postal
era's Bucharest, and a direct link to the [[../events/1812-treaty-of-bucharest|1812
Treaty of Bucharest]]. **Manuc's Inn** (Hanul lui Manuc) was built in **1808** as a
caravanserai by the Armenian merchant **Manuc Bei**; it hosted the preliminary
talks that ended the 1806–1812 Russo-Turkish war [4]. It is the **oldest operating
hotel building in Bucharest** [4].

**Today:** restored and very much alive. After a long restitution trial the inn was
returned in 2007 to the Cantacuzino heirs and refurbished that same year — the yard
paving and wooden galleries redone to bring back its **Brâncovenesc** look — and it
now runs as a hotel, restaurant, bars and coffee-house around the old courtyard
[4][5]. Of every place on this page it is the one a reader can most fully step into.
See [[hanul-lui-manuc]].
